# Literature verification 01: Neyman-Pearson criterion, CFAR, and operating-point reporting

Date: 2026-11-13. Scope: the statistical foundation of the tuning rule "largest share of planted events found, subject to a cap on false calls per hour on negatives".

**Access-level vocabulary used below:** *full text opened* (I extracted and read the passage cited), *table of contents only*, *abstract only*, *could not access*. Anything I did not read is marked. Nothing in the table is from memory unless labelled "from memory, unverified".

## (b) Wording

### Wording we can use (each clause is backed by an opened source)

1. "The tuning rule is a **Neyman-Pearson criterion**: constrain the false-alarm rate and, under that constraint, maximise detection (Neyman & Pearson 1933, Sec. II-III; stated in these terms by Van Trees 1968, Sec. 2.2, p. 33; Lehmann & Romano 2005, Sec. 3.1)."
2. "We borrow the criterion, not the lemma. The Neyman-Pearson lemma says a likelihood-ratio test is most powerful when both distributions are known (Lehmann & Romano 2005, Thm 3.2.1). We make no such claim: the rule only ranks a finite set of candidate settings, using error rates measured on simulated recordings. Using the Neyman-Pearson objective to choose a rule from labelled examples, without known distributions, is an established practice in statistical learning (Scott & Nowak 2005; Tong, Feng & Li 2018)."
3. "Radar CFAR detectors pursue the same objective when the background level is unknown: the threshold is a multiple of a noise estimate taken from cells around the cell under test, with the multiplier chosen for the required false-alarm probability (Finn & Johnson 1968; Rohling 1983; Gandhi & Kassam 1988), and detectors are compared by detection probability at that design false-alarm probability."
4. "Reporting detection at a stated false-alarm rate, rather than one combined score, is the convention in radar (Rohling 1983, Sec. II) and in NIST detection evaluations: 'a single performance number is inadequate' when two error types trade off (Martin et al. 1997). F-score also changes with the proportion of positives even when the detector does not (Fawcett 2006, Sec. 4.2)."
5. Say "false calls per hour" explicitly and call it a **rate**; describe the cap as "measured on simulated negatives".

### Wording we must avoid

1. **"Neyman-Pearson optimal", "most powerful", "optimal detector", "by the Neyman-Pearson lemma".** We have no likelihood ratio and no known distributions. The lemma does not apply to choosing among a grid of settings.
2. **"Finn & Johnson / Rohling / Gandhi & Kassam built CFAR on the Neyman-Pearson criterion"** or any sentence that cites those three papers for the NP link. None of them contains the words "Neyman" or "Pearson" (full-text search of all three). The link is made by textbooks (Richards Ch. 6 then Ch. 7; body text not yet read). Until Richards is read, write "CFAR pursues the same constrained objective" and cite NP sources and CFAR sources separately.
3. **Any sentence saying that Neyman and Pearson (1933) defined the criterion as maximising the probability of detection subject to a bound on the probability of false alarm.** The 1933 paper speaks of errors of the first and second kind and best critical regions. Say "in the detection-theory restatement (Van Trees 1968)".
4. **"The false-alarm probability is held below X"** when what we have is an *empirical* count per hour on a finite set of simulated negatives. Say "at most X false calls per hour on the simulated negatives". An empirical cap is not a guarantee on the population rate (Tong et al. 2018), and not a guarantee on real recordings at all.
5. **"The field has abandoned F1"** or "F1 is invalid for detection". SzCORE (2024) reports F1 beside sensitivity and false alarms per day; Chicco & Jurman replace F1 with another single score. The defensible claim is narrower: a combined score hides the operating point and moves with event prevalence.
6. **"Constant false alarm rate"** for our own tuned detectors unless that property has been shown for them. Our tuning *caps* false calls on a fixed simulated set; CFAR means the rate stays constant as the background level changes. Those are different properties (the library's radar README already notes one of our detectors uses an additive threshold that breaks the CFAR property).
7. "CFAR is the Neyman-Pearson detector." It is what replaces the NP fixed-threshold detector when noise power is unknown, at a quantified loss (Gandhi & Kassam 1988, Sec. II).

## (d) What contradicts or complicates the claims

1. **The three CFAR primaries never mention Neyman-Pearson.** Verified by full-text search. They hold the false-alarm probability constant and report detection at that level, so the *practice* matches the criterion, but the *attribution* is ours (and the textbooks'), not theirs. Claim 2 as worded ("CFAR is built on that criterion") is supported only structurally by Richards' table of contents and by vendor documentation. Gandhi & Kassam come closest ("optimum CFAR processor (maximizes detection probability)").

2. **CFAR is defined in contrast to the NP detector as much as in descent from it.** Gandhi & Kassam (Sec. II) and the MathWorks page both present the NP/"optimum" detector as the fixed-threshold, known-noise case, and CFAR as the practical substitute that pays a "CFAR loss". This helps claim 4 (the engineering tradition itself keeps the constraint and drops optimality) but it means "CFAR implements Neyman-Pearson" is too strong.

3. **An empirical false-alarm cap does not bound the true false-alarm rate.** Tong, Feng & Li (2018) make this their central point: thresholding so that the *empirical* type I error is <= alpha leaves a large probability that the population type I error exceeds alpha (in their simulation "only approximately half of these classifiers have type I errors below" alpha). Our rule caps false calls per hour measured on a finite set of simulated negatives, then *selects the maximum* over candidate settings, which adds a winner's-curse bias on the detection side as well. Scott & Nowak's NP-ERM handles this with an explicit tolerance term. The chapter should either report an interval on the false-call rate, validate the chosen setting on negatives not used for selection, or say plainly that the cap is a property of the tuning set.

4. **Rate per hour is not a probability.** The NP criterion bounds a probability per test. "False calls per hour" is a count per unit time, equal to a per-test probability times the number of tests per hour only if the tests are well defined and comparable. This is not a departure from radar practice (Finn & Johnson, p. 415, motivate the whole method by false alarms exceeding "the radar data-handling rate"; Rohling says "false alarm rate" and P_fa interchangeably), but the chapter should say the constraint is on a rate and name the unit. If candidate settings differ in window length or step, the number of implicit tests per hour differs between them, so equal rates do not mean equal per-test probabilities.

5. **The rare-event literature partly argues the other way.** Davis & Goadrich (2006) and Saito & Rehmsmeier (2015) argue that under heavy class imbalance a per-negative false-positive *rate* can look excellent while false positives swamp true positives, and recommend precision-recall. This is a real objection to "P_D at fixed P_FA" if P_FA is a per-negative probability. It is much weaker against a cap in absolute false calls per hour, which is a count and is what a reader needs to compute precision for any assumed true-event rate. Suggest the chapter state, for the chosen cap, the implied precision at one or two plausible true-event rates. That answers the PR-curve school directly.

6. **Critiques of F1 do not converge on operating points.** Fawcett (2006): F-score depends on class distribution. Chicco & Jurman (2020): F1 ignores true negatives; use MCC (another single score). Hand & Christen (2018, not read): F weights precision and recall differently per method. SzCORE (2024): reports F1 together with sensitivity and false alarms per day. So "F1 is the wrong score" has support; "the accepted alternative is an operating point" is the convention of radar, NIST detection evaluations (Martin et al. 1997) and some security work (Carlini et al. 2022), not a universal one. Also note the Chicco & Jurman "ignores true negatives" point has little force for event detection in continuous time, where true negatives are not countable anyway; the stronger argument for us is Fawcett's (prevalence dependence) and Martin et al.'s (two error types need two numbers).

7. **Equality vs. inequality.** Neyman & Pearson (1933) fix P0(w) = epsilon; Van Trees writes P_F = alpha' <= alpha; Lehmann & Romano bound it by alpha. For a finite grid of settings the constraint will generally not be met with equality, so "subject to a cap" (inequality) is the right form and Van Trees/Lehmann & Romano are the right citations for it.

8. **The investigator chooses the cap.** Neyman & Pearson (p. 296) say explicitly that how the balance between the two errors is struck "must be left to the investigator". The criterion does not tell us what the cap should be; the chapter needs its own justification for the number chosen.

9. **Bibliographic traps found.** Crossref lists the 1933 paper's "issue" as 694-706; those are issue numbers, the pages are 289-337. The library README already records that web sources mis-name Finn's initials (H. M., not R. S.). Chicco & Jurman's text mis-cites Hand & Christen as "Hand and Peter". Richards' chapter numbering was taken from an undated table of contents and may differ by edition.
