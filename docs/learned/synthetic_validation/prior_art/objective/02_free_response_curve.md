# Literature verification 02: the free-response ROC (FROC) paradigm

Date of checks: 2026-11-13.

## How to read this report

- **Access levels** are exact: "full text opened", "abstract only", "could not access".
- **Quotation caveat.** Most pages were read through a fetch tool that returns short excerpts (125 characters or fewer) picked by a small model. Treat quoted fragments as locators, and re-read the passage before quoting it in the chapter. Two sources were read directly as extracted text by me: Setio et al. 2017 (arXiv v4 PDF) and Ziyabari et al. 2018 (arXiv PDF).
- **PubMed attribution.** Abstracts and metadata marked "PubMed" were retrieved from PubMed; DOIs are given as links.
- **Local library.** `01-lit` exists (1106 files). A read-only search of file names for FROC, free-response, Chakraborty, Bunch, Petrick, Kallergi, Setio, LUNA, observer, Egan, false-alarm and spike-detect found nothing. File contents were not searched. Nothing was written there.

## Headline corrections

1. **"FROC was introduced in 1978" is wrong as stated.** The free-response method is Egan, Greenberg & Schulman 1961, and the FROC curve is Miller 1969, both in auditory detection over continuous time. Bunch et al. brought it to imaging; their SPIE paper is dated December 1977 and the journal version 1978.
2. **Our setting is closer to the original than to imaging.** Egan 1961 and Miller 1969 scored responses to signals occurring at arbitrary times in a long listening interval. That is a time-series task with a false-alarm rate, which is our case.
3. **I found no EEG or other time-series paper that calls its sensitivity versus false-alarms-per-hour plot "FROC".** The practice is common; the name is not used there.

## (b) Wording

### Use

- "Free-response scoring: the fraction of planted events localized, against the mean number of false calls per hour of recording."
- "The method of free response was introduced for detecting signals at arbitrary times in a continuous listening interval (Egan et al. 1961); Miller (1969) named the resulting curve FROC; Bunch et al. (1978) carried it into medical imaging, where it is now standard for detection-with-localization tasks."
- "No observation interval is defined, so there is no count of negative cases and no false-positive fraction (Miller 1969)." Add Chakraborty 2006 for "the number of regions that could be mistaken for a target is unknown".
- Map terms once, then keep ours: fraction of planted events found = lesion localization fraction (LLF); false calls per hour = the analogue of non-lesion localization "fraction" (NLF), with an hour of recording in place of an image.
- State the acceptance window in time and the matching rule in the methods, and hold both fixed across detectors. Petrick et al. and Kallergi et al. both ask for this.
- "Fraction found at a fixed false-call rate" for an operating point, citing the LUNA16 convention as precedent for the form of the report.

### Avoid

- "FROC was introduced in 1978." Wrong by 9 to 17 years depending on what is meant.
- "False-positive rate" for false calls per hour. In ROC usage a rate is a fraction of negatives.
- "Sensitivity", "specificity", "true positive", "false positive" as formal terms. Chakraborty's online book (sec. 2.4.1, 2.8) says ROC terms applied to location-level events are not conducive to clarity and discourages them; he uses lesion localization and non-lesion localization. Note the computer-aided-detection literature (Setio, Petrick) does say "sensitivity" and "false positives per scan", so the terms are not wrong, only contested. If we use them, say whose convention.
- "Non-lesion localization fraction" without a gloss. It is a mean count per image and can exceed 1.
- "Area under the FROC curve" as a summary. The abscissa is unbounded; Chakraborty warns against it by name.
- Any claim that our scoring "is FROC analysis". FROC analysis in Chakraborty's sense includes rated marks, case-level figures of merit (AFROC, weighted AFROC) and jackknife inference. We borrow the two axes.
- Citing Petrick et al. for the reason false positives are counted per unit. In what I read, they do not give one.

## (d) What contradicts or complicates the claims

1. **Date and priority.** See the headline corrections. Chakraborty's online book lists Bunch et al. as 1977; his 2013 review and most citations use 1978.
2. **The leading methodologist says not to score with the FROC curve.** Chakraborty's online book (sec. 3.1) says the FROC should never be used to quantify performance. His preferred AFROC and weighted AFROC put a case-level false-positive fraction on the abscissa, computed from the highest-rated false mark on each non-diseased case. Continuous recordings have no such cases unless we cut them into segments, so we cannot follow his recommendation directly. Our two-number report matches the Petrick et al. minimum instead.
3. **Surplus detections on one event.** The imaging convention (Chakraborty sec. 2.4.2; LUNA16) credits the best mark and discards the others. If our one-to-one matching counts the unmatched extras as false calls, we are stricter than the convention and should say so. The EEG literature shows the same choice matters: under the NIST-derived metric in Ziyabari et al., five short detections on one long seizure score as one hit and four or five false positives.
4. **Acceptance window.** A wider window raises the fraction found and lowers false calls at once. Chakraborty 2010 states there is no objective guideline, though he proposes one from the histogram of mark-to-lesion distances. Results at one window do not transfer to another.
5. **"Irrelevant findings".** LUNA16 drops marks on findings that are neither target nor clear error. If our recordings contain real but unplanted coordinated events, detections of them are counted as false calls under our scheme, which inflates the false-call rate relative to that convention.
6. **Operating-point reports hide the curve; partial areas are arbitrary.** The LUNA16 score averages seven chosen points. Chakraborty notes a partial area is undefined for a detector whose curve ends before the chosen limit. The same applies to "fraction found at N false calls per hour" when a detector never produces N per hour.
7. **A rival convention does invent a denominator.** NIST spoken-term detection, as described in Ziyabari et al. sec. 2.1, defines the number of non-target trials as recording duration in seconds minus the number of true occurrences, which yields a false-alarm probability. So "a false-positive fraction is undefined" is true only absent such a convention. Chakraborty calls the image-area analogue "incorrect and naive".
8. **Bunch et al. used only abnormal images** (Chakraborty 2013). The original imaging FROC had no signal-free cases at all, which parallels a recording where every hour may contain events.
9. **Claim 5 is negative on the name.** Searches of PubMed and Europe PMC full text for FROC or free-response together with per-hour false-alarm phrasing and EEG, seizure, spike, spindle or ECG terms returned nothing confirmed. Absence from these searches is not proof of absence; IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar were not searched directly.
