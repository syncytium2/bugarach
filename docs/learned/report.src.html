The network, layer by layer

Every row exists because something had to be true, not because it is a layer people usually stack. The design is Tony’s: look down a tube as the recording slides past; when cells align, a bright spot crosses the centre.

Layer 3 is the whole idea. A difference of Gaussians is excitatory in the middle and inhibitory around it, area-matched so a uniform field sums to zero. Raise the background everywhere and the response does not move; concentrate the same activity into a moment and it spikes. That is “observed minus local context” — the quantity every one of the six detectors computes by hand — written as one convolution and then fitted rather than configured.

It also explains the two behaviours Tony predicted from the tube. Jitter dims the spot, because spreading the onsets pushes them out of the centre and into the surround. A busier background does not, because the surround rises with it.

What the model learned about the data

The centre widths are free parameters, and they were all initialised at the finest thing the recording can resolve — one sample. Where they ended up is the model’s own estimate of how wide a coordinated event is:

Independently, the assessor measures within-event onset spread at 0.39 s and the full footprint of a planted event at 0.80 s. The two middle scales landed at 0.42 s and 0.65 s — the model found the right range having been told only what one sample is.

How far that goes is now measured, and it is less far than it looks. Retraining on a quieter background — identical events, only the background changed — moved the fitted widths to 1.73, 2.96, 5.12, 8.61 samples. The middle scale shifted by 40% while the thing it supposedly measures did not move at all. So the widths are partly absorbing background rather than purely measuring the event, and the honest claim is that they land in the right range, not that they recover the timescale.
That is the property that had to hold. An earlier draft handed the model a bank of fixed time-scales centred on the width we had already measured — the answer written into the input. Starting every scale at one sample and letting it grow is what makes the fitted width a measurement instead of an assumption, and it is why these numbers can be compared to the assessor at all.

The best any of the six manages is recall 0.62. This model reaches 0.80 while still returning four detections per real event, and 0.89 if false alarms are cheap.

Recall at the participant floor is where the six measurably fall over, and it is the reason a learned detector was worth trying. A user who cares about missing events rather than about a balanced score would run it at the other end. F1 does not know that, which is why both points are reported.

Does it survive a change of background?

The claim behind centre-surround is that a uniform change in background cancels. That is testable: train in one regime, deploy in the other, carry the threshold over unchanged. Both regimes are untreated — the quarter and three-quarter points of the per-cell rate spread across real baseline slices, a 4.6-fold change slices show among themselves.

Half the claim survives. Deploying from the busier regime into the quieter one costs essentially nothing — precision even rises. Going the other way costs 0.13 of precision.

The asymmetry says what the mechanism actually does: centre-surround cancels the mean, not the variance. A uniform rise in background does subtract out in expectation, but the fluctuations around it grow with the rate, and those produce chance alignments no surround can remove. A model trained where background is sparse has simply never seen enough of them.

So: train on the busier regime and deploy downward, or train across both. That is a deployable rule, and it came out of the test rather than out of the design.
What this test cannot yet do. The six hand-written detectors show no precision collapse across the same shift — four of them score better on the busier regime. The historical failure this guard exists to catch (precision 90→45, 74→10 when a dense-tuned detector met sparse data) does not reproduce here, so the axis as configured is milder than the one that caused it. And the two models that never learned show a change of exactly zero — not robustness, but a metric with no dynamic range left to lose.

Why building the invariant in beats hoping for it

Before this one, two models were built and neither learned anything: F1 0.12 and 0.15, with loss oscillating between 0.8 and 1.9 for nine hundred steps. They were not broken — given a single window containing an event, they fit it — and four other explanations were tested and eliminated.

The difference is where rate invariance comes from. Those models were given a 2,000-sample receptive field and left to discover that activity should be judged against its local background. Centre-surround does not discover it; it is that operation. Building the invariant in rather than hoping for it is worth more here than any amount of capacity — the model that works is half the size and forty times faster to train.

The measurement this rests on

None of the above would mean anything without a way to parameterize the generator from a real recording, and doing it with the six detectors would make every simulated recording a restatement of whichever detector measured it.

The assessor avoids that: it has no operating point, only a comparison against a null built by circularly shifting each cell within the window — which holds every cell’s own rate and destroys only cross-cell timing. It was MATLAB-only, which broke the per-lab loop at its first step. It is now Python, at 1e‑9 against the original.

What I would do next

  1. Sweep the mass axis, which is now worth running. The number of rate bands the cells are pooled into was the question the whole exercise was built to answer, and it was blocked while every model scored 0.12. This model pools all cells into one band; splitting them is the next knob.
  2. Attack precision, not recall. The model finds events; it also fires on things that are not events. The generator already plants the negatives that would teach it the difference — correlated bursts that are real coincidence, and a dense block containing nothing — and they are currently only labelled, never emphasised.
  3. Vary the sampling rate across training recordings. Every bench recording shares one imaging rate, so nothing here can fail if the model has learned our microscope. The learned centre widths make this testable for the first time: train at one rate, evaluate at another, and see whether they track.
  4. Then the per-lab loop end to end — assess a real recording, parameterize, train, and compare what the model finds against what the assessor measured on the same slice.

Two things I would like decided

Does the generator revision land first? It is in flight and moves the distribution this loop is fitted to. Every number here would need re-running.

Who picks K? It changes the derived event frequency roughly tenfold across its scan, a lab has to choose one, and there is no review surface for that yet.
