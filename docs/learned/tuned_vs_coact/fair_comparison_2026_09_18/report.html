Do the learned detectors beat the hand-written ones once both are tuned fairly?

Simulated recordings, baseline periods, the fast stream (terms below; limits in section 11).

The question. This project detects coordinated events, moments when several cells fire together, in calcium-imaging recordings. It has six hand-written ("coded") detectors and a family of neural networks ("nets") trained to do the same job. An earlier comparison put the best net +0.103 F1 (a 0-to-1 accuracy score, defined below) ahead of the coded detector CoactDetect, but on a different, since-retired simulator, with every net at one untuned setting while CoactDetect had one of its settings tuned. This run tunes both sides, on the current simulator, choosing every setting without looking at the recordings it is scored on (section 6 names the two places the page does look), and asks again.

The answer. Held to the same limit on false alarms, a limit set at 1.6 times CoactDetect's own rates, the hand-written CoactDetect is ahead of every net in every fold of two independent sets of simulated recordings. Chosen for accuracy alone, the two are nearly tied. Counting every training of every net, CoactDetect is ahead on average in both sets; but in this run the best net moves ahead when both sides use the same merge gap, how close two calls must be before they are merged into one, a setting only the coded side was allowed to tune; and in the second set it is ahead in most folds, its average pulled down by two trainings that failed. Margins this small are at the limit of what the scoring resolves. The nets were also handicapped: each fits only 10 of the 72 training recordings, and in this run tuning moved them by a few hundredths of F1 at most, not always up. The earlier lead is gone even for the untuned nets, so it was not lost to tuning the nets; the simulator and CoactDetect's own tuning changed together, and this run cannot separate them. The two sides also win in different places: chosen for accuracy alone, the two chorus nets find more of the faintest events, those joined by only a tenth of the cells, when the cells' background firing is high, and CoactDetect finds more of them when it is low. Among the coded detectors, binned SCE beats CoactDetect under the limit in a few folds, by a few hundredths of F1 (section 9). Sections 6 to 10 give the numbers.

Terms. An ROI (region of interest) is one imaged cell. A cell's firing is one entry in its list of event times; a coordinated event is several ROIs firing together. A call is a detector's claim that a coordinated event happened, over a span of time. F1, from 0 to 1, is the harmonic mean of recall (the share of planted events found) and precision (the share of calls that were real). Recordings are split into four outer folds; a configuration is chosen on three and scored on the fourth, the held-out fold (section 4.1). A refit is one training of a chosen net; each is refitted five times, and a few of those trainings fail outright (section 6). The merge gap is how close two calls may be before they are merged into one (section 2). The budget is the shared limit on false alarms (section 4.4). A draw is one set of simulated recordings; this run's and a second workstation's are independent (section 5). The quiet and busy backgrounds are the two steady rates at which the simulated cells fire at random (section 2). Each recording comes with two event streams, lists of per-cell event times extracted by the lab: the fast stream, from brief calcium transients of about a second, and the slow stream, from much slower ones. Baseline periods are the stretches recorded before any drug was applied. CoactDetect and LoCo are two of the coded detectors (Table 1). Goal 1 is an earlier study in this program that tuned the six coded detectors alone and settled the values this run starts CoactDetect and LoCo from (section 4.3). The project lead is the scientist who owns this project and made the design decisions cited below.

1. The problem: finding coordinated events

A coordinated event is several ROIs firing within a fraction of a second of one another, more often than chance would put them together. Two things make that hard. The cells fire at very different rates, so a busy cell lands near others by accident all the time. And the whole field sometimes becomes busier for minutes at a stretch, which raises chance coincidences everywhere without anything coordinated happening. A detector has to find the few real events without calling either.

A real recording has no answer key, so the example here is simulated (section 2): Figure 1, one simulated recording. It holds planted coordinated events; distractors, bursts built like planted events but labeled as negatives (section 2); and the probe, five minutes in which every ROI fires more often, with nothing planted in it, standing in for the stretches when the whole field becomes busier.

2. Why a simulation, and what it simulates

Nobody knows for certain which moments of a real recording were coordinated, so F1 cannot be computed on one. The project's bench is a simulator whose inputs are measured from real baseline recordings, so its recordings look like real ones, but every planted event is known.

Each bench recording is 45 minutes of 33 ROIs. It holds 15 planted events, 5 at each of three participation levels: 30%, 18% and 10% of the ROIs. Each event's onsets are drawn with a standard deviation of 0.36 s, and events are at least 120 s apart. Every recording seed (the random seed that generates one recording) is simulated at two backgrounds, the steady rates at which every cell also fires at random: the quiet background, 0.0052 firings per second per ROI, and the busy background, 0.019. These are the 25th and 75th percentiles of the rates in real baseline recordings, and they last the whole recording; they are not the probe, which adds 0.06 firings per second per ROI for five minutes (about 12 times the quiet background's rate, and 3 times the busy one's). A detector's score is its F1 pooled within each background, then averaged over the two, so doing well at only one background is not enough.

Each recording also holds 6 distractors. A distractor stands for a real but uncoordinated burst: it is built exactly as a planted event joined by 18% of the ROIs is built, though placed at random times with no regard to the planted events, and is labeled a negative, so a call on one counts against the detector. No detector can tell the two apart by construction, so a detector that finds nearly every 18% event also calls nearly every distractor, and its precision cannot rise far above 0.71 (15 planted events against 6 distractors). Whether real coincidence of that kind should count against a detector is an open question in this project.

Figure 2, how a call is scored, shows the rules. A call hits a planted event if its span, widened by 2.5 s on each side, reaches the event. Matching is one to one: each call can hit at most one event and each event can be hit by at most one call, so a long call over two events finds one of them. Calls inside the probe are counted apart, as false alarms per hour, and kept out of precision. 2.5 s is where the coded detectors' scores stop changing as the tolerance widens; the nets' tolerance curve was never measured.

How fine a difference the scorer can resolve. The scorer's own documentation says to read the order in which it ranks detectors, never the decimal places of one score. On F1 alone, in this run, the margins between the best net and CoactDetect in sections 6 and 7 are 0.007 to 0.010 F1: they say the two are close, and the sign of so small a margin says little.

Every detector here produces calls, and calls closer together than a merge gap are merged into one (Figure 3, merging calls). On this bench, planted events are at least 120 s apart, so for a detector whose calls come in short bursts around each event, a wide merge folds the burst into one call: precision rises and the event is still hit. Where events are a few seconds apart, the same merge fuses them, and with one-to-one matching a fused call finds only one. Merging also chains: calls each within the gap of the next can fuse across far more than the gap. How wide a merge gap each side was allowed is the subject of section 7.

This run used 96 recordings with planted events (48 seeds, 1000 to 1047, at both backgrounds), and 96 empty recordings, one per seed at each background with nothing planted, to count false alarms. The recordings are sampled at 10 frames a second, a frame being 0.1 s.

3. The contestants

Ten detectors, four learned and six coded (Table 1). The nets are known by their names in the code; Table 1 gives each a plain name too. Three of them led the earlier untuned comparison. The fourth, tube, the pool-first net, was kept as a control because it tied CoactDetect there. On this simulator it no longer ties: untuned, it scores 0.629 against the tuned CoactDetect's 0.748.

4. How the comparison was kept fair

4.1 Nested cross-validation: choose on some recordings, score on others

If a setting is chosen by looking at the recordings it is then scored on, the score flatters it. Nested cross-validation prevents that; Varma and Simon (2006, BMC Bioinformatics 7:91) measured how much choosing and scoring on the same recordings flatters a result. The 48 recording seeds are split into 4 outer folds of 12. Each fold takes one turn being held out. With it set aside, every candidate configuration is judged on the other three folds alone, and the winner is scored once on the held-out fold (Figure 4, the nested layout).

The two sides are judged on those three folds differently, because only the nets have anything to fit. A coded detector has no parameters to learn, so each candidate is scored once on all three training folds pooled. A net is fitted, so each candidate is fitted on two training folds and scored on the third, three ways round (the inner loop), at 3 training seeds. A training seed sets a net's random start and also which 10 of the training recordings it fits (section 4.2). An inner fit depends only on its pair of training folds, so each is shared by the two outer folds that train on that pair. The chosen net is then refitted at 5 training seeds and scored on the held-out fold.

Each net had 24 configurations: its untuned one and 23 drawn at random before the run started, from a grid over its learning rate (0.003, 0.01, 0.03), its training steps (900, 1,800, 3,600), and 3 or 4 parameters of its own architecture. Each coded detector's search changed one parameter at a time through goal 1's declared grids (Table 1). One rule removed some grid values: a context window (the stretch a coded detector uses to estimate chance) may not be wider than the 120 s between planted events, because a wider one takes its estimate of chance from a stretch holding other planted events (CoactDetect lost 240 s; LoCo lost 240 s and 480 s; rate+context lost 240 s).

What four folds can and cannot show. The t values below rank how consistent a difference is across folds; they are not tests. Every comparison pairs the two sides fold by fold, so it rests on 4 numbers and 3 degrees of freedom, and each training fold serves three held-out folds, so the folds are less independent than a paired t assumes and the plain t overstates the evidence. Table 2 therefore also gives each t multiplied by 0.655 (√(3/7)), the correction of Nadeau and Bengio (2003, Machine Learning 52:239–281, doi:10.1023/A:1024068626366) for a test set one third the size of the training set. It was derived for repeated random splits; Bouckaert and Frank (2004, PAKDD, LNCS 3056:3–12) carry it to repeated k-fold cross-validation, treating k-fold as a special case of random subsampling; and Bengio and Grandvalet (2004, JMLR 5:1089–1105) show that no unbiased estimator of k-fold's variance holds for every distribution. The correction also has no stated form for a learner that fits only part of its training set, as the nets do, so it is not exact for them either. For comparison, a two-sided 5% test at 3 degrees of freedom would need |t| of at least 3.18.

4.2 A defect fixed before the run: outer folds were training the same model

A net does not train on every training recording. It fits a run of 10 consecutive recordings from the list it is given, the training seed deciding where in the list the run starts, and, when chosen on F1 alone, picks its threshold on the last two recordings of the list. Until this run the list was in fold order, so at some training seeds the run of 10 fell inside the first training fold, and held-out folds sharing that fold would have trained exactly the same model. Four outer folds would then have been fewer than four independent fits (Figure 5, the fitting draws before and after). The 10 is inherited from the earlier comparison, not chosen for this one.

The fix deals the recordings across the training folds in turn, starting after the held-out one, so every fit reaches every training fold and the threshold recordings rotate too. Before launching, the run replayed the exact draws for every training seed, and would have refused to start if any two outer folds shared a fitting set or threshold recordings. "Not the same" is not "independent": at the first training seed, two outer folds' fitting sets share 2 to 3 of their 5 recording seeds.

4.3 The coded side runs in its better mode

CoactDetect and LoCo can count firings in two ways. Binned mode counts them in fixed time bins; sliding mode counts them in a window that moves smoothly (Figure 6, binned and sliding counting). Goal 1 found sliding mode better on held-out recordings, and found that it keeps its calls when a recording is shifted by a fraction of a second, where binned mode can lose them. The project's current defaults are still binned, so every parameter these two searches did not vary was held at goal 1's sliding values instead. (The detector named binned SCE is a different detector, whose only counting is in bins.)

The reference CoactDetect below therefore uses goal 1's values: alpha 10−5, a threshold on an approximate p-value that works as a tuning parameter rather than a calibrated false-alarm rate; a context window of 120 s; a merge gap of 8 s; and a guard of 1 s, the stretch next to the moment tested that is left out of the estimate of chance. LoCo calls when its count passes the 99.9th percentile of the same count on copies with each ROI's firings shifted in time, and uses an 8 s merge gap. The other four coded detectors start from their shipped operating points, the values the project ships them at.

4.4 Two selections, and one false-alarm budget for everyone

Choosing on F1 alone rewards a detector that calls more: extra calls can pick up extra planted events faster than they cost precision. So every contestant was chosen twice (Figure 7, the two selections): once on F1 alone, asking which configuration finds the most events; and once on F1 among the configurations within a shared false-alarm budget, asking which finds the most while firing no more than 1.6 times as often as the reference CoactDetect.

On each outer fold's training recordings, a candidate may fire at most 1.6 times as often as the reference does, on each of three gates: the probe at the quiet background, the probe at the busy background, and the empty recordings at the quiet background. The empty recordings at the busy background are reported but do not gate, by the project lead's decision before the run (goal 2's decision 3); section 10 gives their rates. The limit is shared in name but shaped like CoactDetect: it is the reference's own rate on each gate, scaled, so a detector whose false alarms fall differently across the gates is held by its worst one. 1.6 is a margin the project lead declared before the run, not a measured value, and no sensitivity to it was run. The budget has a floor of one false alarm in the time measured, and no fold's budget was low enough to reach it. It counts calls after merging, so a detector with a wider merge gap calls less and passes it more easily (section 7).

For a net, the two selections choose from the same fits. Under the budget its threshold is chosen inside the budget too, on the inner fits, one threshold for all five refits; on F1 alone each refit picks its own threshold on two recordings. For a coded detector the two selections are separate searches: the budget changes which steps the search can take.

4.5 Which results count: the crowded-recording check

Goal 1 found that a search free to widen the merge gap (Figure 3) gains F1 on this bench by fusing calls, a gain that becomes a loss where events are close together. It added a rule, the crowded-recording check: a coded configuration may not score more than 0.02 F1 below the one it replaces on crowded recordings, where "the one it replaces" is the configuration the search started from (section 9 also scores it against the shipped one). The crowded recordings are 24 simulated recordings of 3 hours with 180 planted events each, at least 6 s apart (the minimum gap in the most crowded real recordings) and 43 s apart at the median: more crowded overall than any real recording, on purpose. It penalizes a merge gap only where the gap fuses events there, which for a detector that merges calls by their event times means a gap wider than about 6 s, and for one that merges sliding windows a narrower one, by about the window's width. 0.02 is a judgment the project lead has not yet signed.

This run's search did not apply the check; it was applied to every coded choice after the run. Goal 1 applies it inside the search, where a refused candidate sends the search elsewhere; applied afterwards, it can strike a choice but cannot find one that passes. A coded result is admissible when it was chosen within the budget and passes the check; a choice on F1 alone is not budgeted, so for it the page says only whether it passes the check. The nets were not checked on crowded recordings: their merge gap was fixed, so their search could not choose a wide one.

4.6 What was not made equal

These asymmetries remain, and each bears on the results below:

  • The merge gap was searched for the coded detectors and fixed at 2 s for the nets (section 7).
  • Training data: a net fits 10 of the 72 training recordings and, on F1 alone, picks its threshold on 2 of them; a coded configuration is scored on all 72 (section 4.2). This handicaps the nets.
  • The crowded-recording check was applied to the coded side only (section 4.5).
  • The budget is CoactDetect's own rate, gate by gate (section 4.4).
  • What the nets were trained on: every training recording contains the same probe and six distractors, labeled as negatives, and distractors are built exactly as planted events joined by 18% of the ROIs are. The nets were taught to refuse those patterns; the coded detectors were only scored on them (section 11).

5. A second, independent draw of recordings

Four outer folds give only 3 degrees of freedom, and a margin of a hundredth of F1 can come and go with which recordings happened to be drawn. So a second workstation ran the same comparison on a disjoint set of recordings: recording seeds 2000 to 2047 instead of 1000 to 1047, with the configurations, grids, training seeds, budget rule and simulator held fixed. A difference between the two runs is then a difference between draws of recordings and between machines, which this comparison cannot separate: the replicate trained on another GPU (graphics processor) of the same model, and retraining on another machine can move a net's F1 by about as much as the margins below (section 11). Its numbers appear beside this run's in section 6, read from its results by the same rules; its own report is the authority on it (section 12).

7. The setting the two sides did not share: the merge gap

On F1 alone, given the same merge gap, the best net moves ahead of CoactDetect in this run, by about 0.01 F1; under the budget, CoactDetect stays ahead at every gap tried. Neither margin is large enough to call. The coded detectors' searches tuned their merge gaps (Figure 3); the nets' was fixed at 2 s (20 frames), the default returned by their threshold picker (the training step that sets a net's threshold), and never searched. The budget counts calls after merging (section 4.4), so the difference touches both selections.

Figure 10, the chorus nets against CoactDetect at matched gaps, re-scores both sides with only the merge gap changed. On F1 alone, chorus_norm is ahead by +0.010 with both at 2 s (4 of 4 folds; t 2.8, corrected 1.9) and by +0.009 with both at 8 s (3 of 4; t 2.3, corrected 1.5). Under the budget, CoactDetect stays ahead of chorus_gain_norm in every fold at every matched gap (t −7.1 to −4.3), and of chorus_norm by 0.016 to 0.020 F1, with the net ahead in at most 1 of 4 folds (t −2.1 to −1.5): as weak as the reversal on F1 alone. Each matched comparison handicaps one side. At 2 s, CoactDetect's other parameters were tuned together with an 8 s gap. At a wider gap, a net keeps a threshold chosen at 2 s; under the budget that handicap favors CoactDetect, since a wider merge calls less and a lower threshold might then have fitted the budget.

Figure 11, what the merge gap is worth, shows each side's held-out F1 as the gap changes, on F1 alone. A wider merge helps every coded detector shown: CoactDetect rises from 0.731 at 2 s to 0.748 at its chosen 8 s and 0.803 at 30 s. Among the nets it helps chorus_norm and chorus_gain_norm: chorus_norm goes from 0.741 at 2 s to 0.775 at 16 s. On F1 alone it costs line_length and tube, whose worst refits call almost continuously at a threshold near the bottom of the grid, so merging fuses their calls into long spans; under the budget a wider merge helps all four nets. Nothing was re-chosen at a new gap, so these curves indicate what tuning the gap could do; a search that tuned it together with the other parameters could do better.

A rerun that tunes the nets' merge gap like any other parameter would remove the asymmetry; it needs no retraining, only re-scoring. It has not been run.

8. Where the two sides differ

A pooled F1 can hide two sides winning in different places, and here it does (Figure 12, the planted events found, by participation and background). Events joined by 30% or 18% of the ROIs are found by both sides almost every time: recall 0.79 or more in every fold, background and selection, for CoactDetect and both chorus nets. What separates them is the faintest events, joined by 10% of the ROIs. On F1 alone, against the busy background, chorus_norm finds more of them than CoactDetect in 4 of 4 folds (0.59 against 0.35 on average), and chorus_gain_norm does too, in both selections; against the quiet background CoactDetect finds more in 3 of 4 (0.81 against 0.72). By background, chorus_norm minus CoactDetect in F1 is −0.014 at the quiet background and +0.000 at the busy one: the net's extra faint events at the busy background are paid for in precision, 0.602 against CoactDetect's 0.654 there. Both sides also call 84% to 100% of the distractors on F1 alone, a distractor counting as called when any call's widened span reaches it. The breakdown covers the two chorus nets and this run only.

9. The coded detectors' choices

  • Binned SCE finishes first on paper, at 0.771 on F1 alone, by merging calls up to 30 s apart, and it is no better than CoactDetect at a matched gap. Its search chose a 30 s merge gap, the top of its grid, in every fold; at that same gap CoactDetect scores 0.803 (Figure 11). Its choices fail the crowded-recording check in 7 of 8 cases. The one that passes is fold 4 under the budget, 0.012 F1 below its start on the crowded recordings, inside the 0.02 allowance, and there it is ahead of CoactDetect by +0.030 F1, an admissible win in one fold. In the replicate the pattern is stronger: under the budget, binned SCE's choices pass the replicate's own crowded-recording check in 4 of 4 folds, the budget refused every configuration in 1, and in the 3 admissible folds it is ahead of CoactDetect, by +0.011, +0.014 and +0.017 F1. So under the budget, binned SCE is the one detector on this page that admissibly beats CoactDetect in some folds: 1 of 4 here and 3 of 4 in the replicate, by a few hundredths of F1 at most.
  • Under the budget, LoCo, locust, SPIKE-synch and rate+context have no admissible result in any fold. The budget is anchored to CoactDetect, and every other coded detector's starting point was over it in every fold (binned SCE, LoCo, locust, SPIKE-synch and rate+context). When a start breaks the budget, the search takes the best value within the budget on the first parameter, in the grid's order, that has one, whatever that costs: at fold 1, LoCo moved threshold_pctile from 99.9 to 99.99, training F1 0.73 to 0.68; SPIKE-synch moved C_threshold from 0.1 to 0.16, training F1 0.48 to 0.22; rate+context moved excess_threshold_hz from 4.5 to 8, training F1 0.64 to 0.43. For LoCo, SPIKE-synch and rate+context, what the searches reached that way loses events everywhere, crowded recordings included, and fails the check in every fold. So these results depend on the order of the parameters in the grid as well as on the detectors, and the check, applied after the search, could not look for a configuration that passes both rules (section 4.5). For locust, the budget refused every configuration its search tried, the start included, so it has no result under the budget at all.
  • Which reference the check uses changes no verdicts (Table 3; Figure 13, the coded choices on the crowded recordings).
  • SPIKE-synch, under the budget, dropped the local-gap coincidence window that defines SPIKE-synchronization and used a fixed window in 4 of 4 folds.
  • CoactDetect exceeded the budget on the held-out fold in 1 of 4 folds: a choice made within the budget on training recordings need not stay within it on new ones. No other admissible coded choice exceeded it.

10. Tuning, failed refits and false alarms

Tuning moved the nets by little, and not always up (Figure 14, tuned minus untuned): in this run no net's mean moved by more than 0.04 F1 either way. On F1 alone, tuned minus untuned is chorus_norm +0.005, chorus_gain_norm −0.006, line_length +0.032, tube +0.002 in this run, and chorus_norm −0.063, chorus_gain_norm −0.017, line_length +0.029, tube −0.005 in the replicate, where chorus_norm's figure includes the two refits that failed to train. Under the budget the comparison is not like for like: the tuned configuration is held to the budget and the untuned one is not, so the difference (chorus_norm −0.020, chorus_gain_norm +0.009, line_length +0.023, tube −0.035) mixes what tuning gained with what the budget cost.

The earlier +0.103 lead was not lost to the nets' tuning. Untuned, chorus_norm is already within 0.02 F1 of the tuned CoactDetect on this simulator: −0.012 in this run and +0.006 in the replicate. Two things changed together between the earlier comparison and this one, the simulator and CoactDetect's own tuning (goal 1's sliding values and this run's search), and this run cannot separate them.

Refits below 0.2 F1. Every refit was kept in every mean on this page except where section 6 says otherwise. In this run 2 of 210 distinct refits scored below 0.2 F1 (the 210 are scored 240 times, since each is scored at every selection that chose it), and every other scored at least 0.51; in the replicate, 3 did, all in chorus nets, and its lowest other refit scored 0.39. Table 4 lists them. That is about 1% of refits across the two draws. No rule fixed before seeing the held-out scores picks out exactly these refits: the threshold picker's own warning, raised when it chooses the bottom of its grid, also fires on healthy refits.

False alarms on held-out recordings. The budget binds the nets mainly on the empty recordings at the quiet background, where it allowed 10.4 to 11.5 false alarms per hour: there the chorus nets' refits chosen under the budget fired 0.0 to 22.9, and CoactDetect 5.9 to 8.2. In the probe the same refits fired 0 to 17 per hour (mean of the two backgrounds; median 1), against CoactDetect's 8 to 12.5; across the five refits of one chorus-net choice the probe rate at one background differs by up to 23 false alarms per hour. The nets were trained on recordings containing this same probe, labeled as a negative, so a low probe rate shows they learned that pattern, not that they resist a busier field in general (section 11). On the empty recordings at the busy background, which do not gate, the chorus nets fired 0.0 to 41.2 per hour, CoactDetect 3.4 to 6.1, and binned SCE 22.7 to 32.8. Among the 80 refits of nets chosen under the budget, these exceeded it on the held-out fold:

  • chorus_norm: 4 of 20 (per fold 0, 0, 1, 3).
  • chorus_gain_norm: 7 of 20 (per fold 2, 2, 0, 3).
  • line_length: 3 of 20 (per fold 1, 0, 1, 1).
  • tube: 11 of 20 (per fold 1, 4, 5, 1).

A net over the budget calls more freely than the budget allows, which tends to favor its F1.

11. Limits

  • Baseline periods only, fast stream only. The project lead's decision of 2026-09-17 for this run. The bench is fitted to the baseline periods of the fast stream, so nothing here says how any detector behaves during drug treatment or on the slow stream.
  • The bench's fitted values were measured on data with a known, uncleared contamination. The bench was fitted to an export folder, the lab's exported per-cell event lists. In a few ROIs of that folder, motion correction clamped the signal to its minimum value, and the lab's event extraction recorded firings on the steps in and out of it: about 0.03% of firings. Re-measuring the bench on a corrected folder moved every value by less than its own bootstrap interval (the spread of the value over resampled recordings), a check with little power against an effect that small. Moving the bench to the corrected folder has not been decided. This page lists the contamination as a limit because the project lead asked for exactly that in the brief for this report (2026-09-19); listing it does not show that the effect is negligible.
  • One of the bench's values sits on the edge of its own measured interval, and the decision on it is open. The bench declares its middle participation level as 0.18, which plants 6 of 33 ROIs, 18.18%. The real recordings measure 19.05%, with a 95% interval whose lower end is 18.18%: the planted count sits on the interval's edge and the declared 0.18 just outside it. Changing it would move every bench number, so it waits on the project lead.
  • Simulation only. Every number is on simulated recordings. The bench is measured from real ones, but a detector that wins here has not thereby been shown to win on real data.
  • The budget is CoactDetect's shape and a declared margin. It is 1.6 times the reference CoactDetect's own rate on each gate (section 4.4); the 1.6 was declared, not measured, and no sensitivity to it was run, though every under-budget verdict rests on it.
  • What the nets were trained on. Every training recording holds the same probe, at the same place and rate, and six distractors built exactly as planted events joined by 18% of the ROIs are, and the nets were trained to call neither. So the nets met the probe in training and the coded detectors did not, and the nets were fitted to patterns identical to planted events but labeled the opposite way. The effect of either on the results was not measured.
  • The merge gap was tuned for one side only (section 7), and "matched" is nominal. There are three merge rules: a net merges runs of frames above its threshold; CoactDetect and LoCo merge the positions of a sliding window, so events up to about the gap plus the window's width apart can fuse; binned SCE merges by the times of the firings. The same number of seconds drives different operations.
  • The nets were not checked on crowded recordings. Every net number was measured on events at least 120 s apart.
  • The two sides learn from different amounts of data. A net fits 10 of the 72 training recordings and, on F1 alone, picks its threshold on 2; a coded configuration, threshold included, is scored on all 72. That handicaps the nets. On F1 alone the net's threshold is also picked on F1 pooled over both backgrounds, while every selection and score on this page averages the two backgrounds' F1.
  • The vote-pooling nets standardize each ROI over the whole input. They trained on crops of about 7 minutes and are scored on the whole 45-minute recording, probe included, so the statistics they standardize by differ between training and scoring.
  • The hit tolerance was set for the coded detectors. The 2.5 s at which scores stop changing was measured for the coded detectors at their shipped configurations, not at the tuned ones here and not for the nets.
  • Four outer folds, so every paired comparison rests on 3 degrees of freedom, and the folds share training data (section 4.1).
  • The coded searches move one parameter at a time and can end in different places depending on the path taken; a search under the budget can even end higher than the same search without it, because the budget changes which steps it can take.
  • Chosen values at the top of their grid. On F1 alone every coded detector with a merge gap chose the top of its grid (Table 3). Goal 1 had tried CoactDetect's and LoCo's merge gap at 16 s and its crowded check refused it, so 8 s is the most those two could take. SPIKE-synch's longest allowed gap within an event and locust's minimum distance between calls, both merge-like, also sit at the top of their grids on F1 alone.
  • Tuning the fewest ROIs a coded detector will call an event on (min_rois) can fit it to the bench's planted participation levels, so its tuned value is a fact about this simulator.
  • One training run per seed, on one GPU per workstation. Re-scoring on a CPU moved nets by up to 0.0015 F1 per refit (section 7). Only one earlier check compared training on two machines, and the second machine had uncommitted changes, so its difference of up to about 0.02 F1 per fold mixes machine and code; that is the size of the margins above.
