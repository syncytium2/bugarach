Do the learned detectors beat the hand-written ones once both are tuned fairly?

Simulated recordings, baseline periods, the fast stream (section 7).

The question. This project detects coordinated events, moments when several cells fire together, in calcium-imaging recordings. It has six hand-written ("coded") detectors and a family of neural networks ("nets") trained to do the same job. An earlier comparison put the best net +0.103 F1 ahead of the coded detector CoactDetect, but on a different simulator (since retired), with the nets untuned and CoactDetect tuned on one setting. This run tunes both sides, on the current simulator, choosing every setting without looking at the recordings it is scored on, and asks again.

The answer. Once both sides are tuned, the best net and CoactDetect finish within 0.010 F1 of each other, and which one is ahead turns on a setting the run treated unequally: how close two calls must be before they are merged into one. The coded detectors' search tuned it; the nets' was fixed at 2 seconds. At the settings the run chose, 2 seconds for the nets and 8 for CoactDetect, CoactDetect is ahead of the best net, chorus_norm, by 0.007 F1. With the gap matched, chorus_norm is ahead: by +0.010 with both at 2 seconds (4 of 4 folds) and by +0.009 with both at 8 (3 of 4 folds). So this run does not settle which side is better. A second, independent draw of recordings on another workstation agrees that no net is ahead at the settings as chosen; it was not re-scored at matched gaps (section 5). One coded detector, binned SCE, finishes ahead of all of them, and only by merging calls up to 30 seconds apart. On real recordings, where coordinated events can come a few seconds apart, that would fuse separate events (section 4.5).

Terms used throughout: ROI, region of interest, one imaged cell; F1, the harmonic mean of recall (the share of planted events found) and precision (the share of calls that were real), from 0 to 1; GPU, graphics processor; t, the paired t statistic across the four outer folds, on 3 degrees of freedom, read as described in section 6. Goal 1 is an earlier study in this program that tuned the six coded detectors alone, every setting they accept, and settled the values this run starts them from (section 4.3). The project lead is the scientist who owns this project and made the design decisions cited below.

1. The problem: finding coordinated events

Each row of a raster is one ROI; each tick is a moment the cell fired. A coordinated event is several ROIs firing within a fraction of a second of one another, more often than chance would put them together. Two things make that hard. The cells fire at very different rates, so a busy cell lands near others by accident all the time. And the whole field sometimes becomes busier for minutes at a stretch, which raises chance coincidences everywhere without anything coordinated happening. A detector has to find the few real events without firing on either. Figure 1, one simulated recording, shows what a detector meets: a planted event, marked in the lane above the raster; two distractors; and the probe, five minutes in which every ROI fires an extra 0.06 times a second, about 12 times the quiet background rate and 3 times the busy one, with nothing planted in it. A distractor is built exactly as an 18% planted event is built, and is labeled a negative: a call on one counts against the detector. Whether real coincidence of that kind should count against a detector is an open question in this project; here it counts the same way for every contestant.

2. Why a simulation, and what it simulates

A real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which moments were coordinated, so F1 cannot be computed on it. The project's bench is a simulator whose settings are measured from real baseline recordings, so its recordings look like real ones, but every planted event is known.

Each bench recording is 45 minutes of 33 ROIs. It holds 15 planted events, 5 at each of three participation levels: 30%, 18% and 10% of the ROIs. Each event's onsets are spread by 0.36 s, and events are at least 120 s apart. It also holds 6 distractors and the probe.

Every recording seed (the random seed that generates one recording) is simulated at two backgrounds: 0.0052 and 0.019 events per second per ROI. These are the 25th and 75th percentiles of the rates in real baseline recordings. A detector's score is its F1 pooled within each background, then averaged over the two, so doing well at only one background is not enough.

A call is a hit if its time span, widened by 2.5 s, contains a planted event. 2.5 s is where the scores stop changing as the tolerance widens. One consequence matters later: a call that spans a long stretch hits any event inside it. Calls inside the probe are counted separately, as false alarms per hour, and kept out of precision.

This run used 96 recordings with planted events (48 seeds, 1000–1047, at both backgrounds), plus 96 with nothing planted (one per seed at each background), used to count false alarms.

3. The contestants

Ten detectors, four learned and six coded (Table 1). The nets are known by their names in the code. Three of them led the earlier untuned comparison. The fourth, tube, was kept as a control because it tied CoactDetect there. On this simulator it no longer ties: untuned, it scores 0.629 against CoactDetect's 0.748.

The difference between the nets that matters most is where each stops treating ROIs separately. tube pools them first. The other three keep each ROI separate long enough for it to cast a bounded vote, then pool the votes. Each net ends in a probability per frame, and its threshold on that probability is chosen on training recordings.

4. How the comparison was kept fair

4.1 Nested cross-validation: choose on some recordings, score on others

If a setting is chosen by looking at the recordings it is then scored on, the score flatters it. Nested cross-validation prevents that (Figure 2, the nested layout). The 48 recording seeds are split into 4 outer folds of 12. Each fold takes one turn being held out. With it set aside, every candidate setting is judged on the other three folds alone. The winner is then scored once on the held-out fold, which is never seen while choosing.

The two sides are judged on those three folds differently, because only the nets have anything to fit. A coded detector has no parameters to learn, so each candidate setting is scored once on all three training folds pooled. A net is fitted, so each candidate is fitted on two training folds and scored on the third, three ways round (the inner loop, panel B), at 3 training seeds (the random start of a net's training, unrelated to recording seeds). The chosen net is then refitted at 5 training seeds and scored on the held-out fold.

Each net had 24 configurations, drawn at random before the run started, from a grid over its learning rate (0.003, 0.01, 0.03), its training steps (900, 1,800, 3,600), and 3 or 4 settings of its own architecture. The untuned setting was always among them. Each coded detector's search moved one setting at a time through goal 1's declared grids (Table 1). Settings that break the context-window rule in section 4.5 were skipped.

4.2 A defect fixed before the run: outer folds were training the same model

A net does not train on every training recording. It fits a run of 10 consecutive recordings from the list it is given, and the last two recordings on the list pick its threshold. Until this run the list was in fold order. At some training seeds the run of 10 then fell inside the first training fold, so held-out folds that share that fold trained exactly the same model. Figure 3, the fitting draws before and after, shows it at training seed 0 on this run's own split: three of the four held-out folds would have fitted the same ten recordings. Four outer folds were then fewer than four independent fits, and a comparison across folds counts independence it does not have.

The fix deals the recordings across the training folds in turn, starting after the held-out one, so every fit reaches every training fold and the threshold recordings rotate too. Before launching, the run replayed the exact draws for every training seed. It would have refused to start if any two outer folds shared a fitting set or threshold recordings.

4.3 The coded side runs in its better mode

For CoactDetect and LoCo, every setting the search did not vary was held at goal 1's values, not at the project's current default settings. The difference is the counting mode. Binned mode counts coincidences in fixed time bins. Sliding mode counts them in a window that moves smoothly. Goal 1 found sliding mode better on held-out recordings. It also found that sliding mode keeps its calls when a recording is shifted by a fraction of a second, where binned mode can lose them. The project's defaults are still binned, so a search built on them would have tuned the coded side in its weaker mode.

The reference CoactDetect below therefore uses goal 1's settings:

  • alpha (the significance level for calling an event) 1e-05;
  • a context window (the stretch used to estimate chance) of 120 s;
  • a merge gap of 8 s;
  • a guard of 1 s (the stretch around an event left out of that estimate).

LoCo uses the 99.9th percentile and an 8 s merge gap.

4.4 Two selections, and one false-alarm budget for everyone

Choosing on F1 alone rewards a detector that calls more: extra calls can pick up extra planted events faster than they cost precision. So every contestant was chosen twice. Once on F1 alone, asking which setting finds the most events. Once on F1 among the settings within a shared false-alarm budget, asking which finds the most while firing no more than CoactDetect does (Figure 4, the two selections).

The budget is anchored to the reference CoactDetect. On each outer fold's training recordings, a candidate may fire at most 1.6 times as often as the reference does, both in the probe at each background and on the quiet recordings with nothing planted. 1.6 is a margin the project lead declared before the run, not a measured value. The budget never drops below one false alarm in the time measured, a floor that bound in no fold.

For a net, the two selections choose from the same fits, and its threshold is chosen inside the budget too. For a coded detector they are two separate searches: the budget changes which steps the search can take.

4.5 One setting the two sides did not share: the merge gap

Every detector here produces calls, and calls closer together than a merge gap are merged into one. On this bench, events are at least 120 s apart. A wide merge gap therefore loses nothing: it folds the near-duplicate calls around one event into one call, which raises precision, and the merged call still hits its event (section 2's hit rule). On real recordings, coordinated events can come a few seconds apart, and a wide merge fuses them (Figure 5, merging and its effect on F1, panel A).

The coded detectors' searches tuned their merge gaps. The nets' was fixed at 2 s, the default their threshold picker returns, and never searched. Panel B shows what the gap is worth on each side. Only the merge gap was changed: every coded choice was rescored, and every chosen net refit was re-decoded from its saved model, keeping its threshold. Both sides first reproduce the run's own scores at their own gaps: the coded detectors exactly, the nets within 0.001 F1. On this bench, a wider merge helps every coded detector shown. CoactDetect rises from 0.731 at 2 s to 0.748 at its chosen 8 s and 0.803 at 30 s. chorus_norm scores 0.741 at its 2 s and 0.775 at its best gap tried, 16 s. Nothing was re-chosen, so these curves bound what tuning the gap could do rather than measure it.

Goal 1 met the same effect and added a rule before this run: the crowded-recording check. A setting may not score more than 0.02 F1 below the setting it replaces on crowded recordings. These are 24 bench recordings with 180 events each, spaced as the crowded end of real recordings is: at least 6 s apart, median 43 s. 0.02 is a judgment the project lead has not yet signed. The run's own search did not apply the check (its declaration does not mention it). It was applied afterwards, when the other workstation pointed at the merge gaps, by tools/crowded_check_fair_comparison.py. That tool uses goal 1's own scoring on 12 crowded recordings per background.

The setting a choice replaces is the one its search started from: goal 1's values for CoactDetect and LoCo, and the project's defaults for the others. Goal 1 compares against the project's shipped defaults instead. Scored against those, no verdict changes. The nets were not checked on crowded recordings; their merge gap is fixed, so their search could not choose a wide one.

5. A second, independent draw of recordings

Four outer folds give only 3 degrees of freedom, and a margin of a hundredth of F1 can come and go with which recordings happened to be drawn. So the workstation WSMIP065 ran the same comparison on a disjoint set of recordings (Figure 6, the two draws): recording seeds 2000–2047 instead of 1000–1047. The configurations, grids, training seeds, budget rule and simulator were held fixed. A difference between the two runs is then a difference between draws of data. This page shows the replicate's headline numbers beside this run's in Figure 8, copied from its results; its own report is the authority on it: <darkroom>/bugarach/2026-09-18-replicate-run-status/ (WSMIP065's report).

6. What came out

How to read the t values first. They are paired t statistics over four folds. Each training fold serves three held-out folds, so the folds are less independent than the statistic assumes, and the plain values overstate the evidence. Table 2 therefore also gives each t multiplied by 0.65, the correction of Nadeau and Bengio 2003 (Machine Learning 52:239–281) for a test set one third the size of its training set.

That correction was derived for repeated random splits, not for k-fold cross-validation. Bouckaert and Frank (2004) carry it over to k-fold as a heuristic, and Bengio and Grandvalet (2004) show that no exact correction exists for k-fold. Read the corrected values as a ranking of consistency, not as tests. At 3 degrees of freedom, a two-sided 5% test would need about 3.2. The second draw of recordings (section 5) is the stronger check.

Figure 7, held-out F1 for all ten contestants, shows every outer fold. Table 2 gives the means and each contestant's difference from CoactDetect.

Refits below 0.2 F1

Of the 210 held-out refits of nets, 2 scored below 0.2 F1; every other refit scored at least 0.51, so the cut falls in an empty stretch of the distribution. Both are included in every mean above:

  • chorus_gain_norm, fold 3, training seed 4 of 5: F1 0.125, in the choice on F1 alone. Its record carries the failed-training signature: it calls one long stretch per recording, so its few hits come at perfect precision and almost no recall.
  • tube, fold 1, training seed 5 of 5: F1 0.000, in the choice on F1 under the budget. It made no calls at all at a threshold picked on two recordings, and a F1 with no calls counts as 0.

Choices that exceeded the budget on new data

A choice made within the budget on the training folds can exceed it on the held-out fold. Among the coded choices under the budget, CoactDetect exceeded it in 1 of 4 folds. Among the 80 held-out refits of nets chosen under the budget, these exceeded it:

  • chorus_norm: 4 of 20 (per fold 0, 0, 1, 3).
  • chorus_gain_norm: 7 of 20 (per fold 2, 2, 0, 3).
  • line_length: 3 of 20 (per fold 1, 0, 1, 1).
  • tube: 11 of 20 (per fold 1, 4, 5, 1).

Exceeding the budget can only help a net's F1 under the budget, so this does not favor the coded side.

7. Limits

  • Baseline periods only, fast stream only. The project lead's decision of 2026-09-17 for this run. The recordings in this project have two event streams (fast and slow), and periods before (baseline) and during drug treatment. The bench is fitted to the baseline periods of the fast stream, so nothing here says how any detector behaves in treatment periods or on the slow stream.
  • The bench's fitted values were measured on data with a known, uncleared contamination. They were last measured on the export folder steps_excluded. In a few ROIs of that folder, motion correction clamped the signal to its minimum value, and the detectors call events on the steps in and out of it: about 0.03% of events (HANDOFF-slow-comodulation-on-the-de-pinned-export.md, decision 3). Both workstations re-measured the bench on the corrected folder on 2026-09-17, and every value moved by less than its own bootstrap interval. That record is commit 2120516 on branch tune-bench-comparison (HANDOFF-workstation-tuning.md), not yet on main. Moving the bench to the corrected folder has not been decided. This page states the contamination as a limit because the project lead's brief for this report asked for exactly that; it is not evidence that the effect is negligible.
  • One of the bench's values sits outside its own measured interval, and the decision on it is open. The run's declaration records it: 2026-09-17, awaiting Tony. The bench holds 0.18; steps_excluded measures 0.1905 (6 median participants over 31.5 median ROIs) with a 95% interval whose lower end is 0.1818, which is 6/33: the ratio BENCH_RECORDING's docstring derives and then rounds to 0.18. A rounding, not a moved measurement, but moving it moves every bench number, so it is not moved here.
  • Simulation only. Every number is on simulated recordings. The bench is measured from real ones, but a detector that wins here has not thereby been shown to win on real data.
  • The merge gap was tuned for one side only (section 4.5). A rerun with the nets' merge gap chosen like any other setting needs no retraining, only re-scoring. It has not been run.
  • The nets were not checked on crowded recordings. The headline was measured on well-spaced events only.
  • The two sides learn from different amounts of data. A net fits 10 of the 72 training recordings and picks its threshold on 2. A coded setting, threshold included, is scored on all 72.
  • Four outer folds, so every paired comparison rests on 3 degrees of freedom, and the folds share training data (section 6).
  • The coded searches move one setting at a time and can end in different places depending on the path. Under the budget, a search can even score higher than without it.
  • Chosen values at the top of their grid mean a search might have gone further. For the merge gap, that is every coded detector with one (Table 3).
  • The fewest ROIs a coded detector will call an event on (min_rois) can learn the bench's planted participation levels, so its tuned value is a fact about this simulator.
  • One training run per seed, on one GPU. A rerun on another GPU or on processors may not reproduce these F1 values exactly; how far they would move is unmeasured.
