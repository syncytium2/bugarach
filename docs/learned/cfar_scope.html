Every cell is judged against
the cells beside it

A fixed threshold cannot survive a background that moves. CFAR detectors throw the fixed bar away and rebuild it at every cell from that cell's own neighbours — so the false alarm rate stays put while the background does not. The variants differ in one thing only: how the neighbouring cells are combined into a single number. Average them and you get CA. Take the greater of the two halves and you get GO. That one choice is the whole design space, and below it is running.

Three things the picture is showing

Guard cells stop a target masking itself

Drag guard cells to 0 and watch the threshold grow a bump exactly where each target is. The target's own energy is sitting in the estimate of the background it is being judged against, so it raises its own bar. Strong targets vanish first.

reference cells “are separated from the CUT by a number of guard cells, whose purpose is to limit the effects of a range spread target” Weinberg, on the standard CFAR layout

By 1983 the practice was settled enough that Rohling specifies “two guard cells directly adjacent to the test cell” as a setup detail, with no argument for it.

Greatest-of is edge-robust and target-blind

On clutter edge, CA and SO drag half a window of quiet cells across the step and fire a burst of false alarms on the loud side. GO takes the greater half, so a transition straddling the test cell can never lower the bar.

“Although the false alarm rate performance of the GO-CFAR processor in regions of clutter transition is better than that of any other mean-level CFAR scheme, the detection performance in the multiple target environment is quite poor.” Gandhi & Kassam, IEEE T-AES 24(4), 1988

Switch to two close targets and watch GO come last. Rohling is blunt about why: “due to symmetry, the splitting of the reference window of the CAGO CFAR does not help in this situation”. The split is the entire mechanism, and against an interferer it contributes nothing.

Every rule is somebody's worst case

Smallest-of is GO read backwards, and it inherits the mirror image: it resolves close targets better than anything else here and it is the worst detector on the page at a clutter edge, by an order of magnitude. Nobody deploys it by accident.

The ordered-cell rules split the difference. OS reads off the k-th smallest, so an interferer high in the window never reaches the bar; TM throws the two loudest away and averages the rest. Both handle a neighbour well and an edge only tolerably — which is Gandhi & Kassam's finding, that ordered-cell schemes have “in general better overall performance” while GO still owns the transition.

And GO's split, when it loses, loses cheaply: Hansen & Sawyers measured the extra loss in stationary clutter at “0.1 to 0.3 dB”.

Two live consequences. All three rolling detectors now take a guard_sec, and all three default it to 0 — so the shipped bar is still the unguarded one, and the slider above is the shape of what that costs. And maxlt should be expected to miss the second of two coordinated events falling inside one half-context (60 s FAST, 30 s SLOW at shipped defaults), because of the mechanism that makes it good at drug onsets — the two close targets environment above, in the units this preparation actually uses. Nothing in either tree tests it yet.

The model here is radar's, not this lab's: square-law envelope, exponential cell statistics, a Swerling-style target that multiplies local clutter power and spreads over ±2 cells the way a matched filter output does. Those skirt cells are scored as neither hit nor false alarm — they are the reason a guard interval exists. It reproduces the shapes of the CFAR argument; the decibels do not transfer to a count of distinct ROIs. That caveat is detector_history §5.4's, and it applies to this page too.
