Everyone finds coordinated events. Almost nobody learns to.

Where a learned coordination detector sits in neuroscience — what the neighbouring fields already do, which of it was reached independently here, and what is actually left to claim. Twelve papers, ten of them read in full.

This page exists because an earlier claim was too strong. A companion report said no prior work trains a network to emit coordinated population events. Four searches deep, no paper opened. Reading them found three such networks, so the claim is withdrawn and replaced with a narrower one that survives contact with the methods sections. The shelf is at <darkroom>/bugarach/lit/coordination/, with per-paper read status, because a citation nobody opened is how the first version went wrong.

0 · The question, stated so it can be answered

Not has anyone used deep learning on calcium imaging — a great many people have, and the useful question is narrower. A coordination analysis takes per-cell activity and asks when a group of cells fired together. So:

Has anyone trained a model whose output is the coordinated population event itself — a time interval, scored against known events — rather than a per-cell spike train, a latent trajectory, or the settings of a classical detector?

That question has four independent parts, and they are worth keeping apart because different fields answer them differently: what the method emits, whether it is trained, where its ground truth comes from, and what it is compared against.

1 · The field, as one picture

Two axes carry most of the structure. What a method emits, left to right: which cell fired, which cells group together, or when the group fired. And how it decides — a rule somebody wrote, or a function fitted to data.

Three features of that picture do the work, and only one of them is flattering.

The middle of the top row is empty, and it stays empty

Nobody trains a network to emit assembly membership. That is not an oversight waiting to be filled — membership is a clustering, and the field solves it with eigenvalue tests and graph community detection that need no labels at all. An empty cell here is a field that does not need the tool, not a gap.

The bottom right is where this project's six detectors live — and so does a step inside three methods from the column to its left

Mölter, Avitan and Goodhill benchmark eight assembly detectors. Three of them — SGC, CORE and SVD — cannot start without first finding events: each binarises per cell, counts coactive cells per frame, and keeps only frames whose coactivity level exceeds the 95th percentile (SVD: the 99th) of a thousand-iteration per-cell permutation null.

That is the same construction as the SCE rule and as CICADA, arrived at independently, sitting as a precondition of assembly detection rather than as anybody's output. It is drawn as the dashed arrow. It also means the most obvious published comparison for this work needs no adapter at all: those high-coactivity frames already are events in the sense our scorer means.

The top right is occupied — and none of it is calcium imaging

DOSED predicts an event's centre, duration and class from raw multichannel EEG. cnn-ripple emits a per-window probability of a hippocampal sharp-wave ripple. SEED does spindles and K-complexes. SpikeNet does epileptiform discharges at what its authors measure as expert level. All four descend from single-shot object detectors — YOLO and SSD, transplanted from vision to one dimension — and all four take their ground truth from human experts.

2 · What that costs the novelty claim, and what is left

Withdrawn. “No prior work trains a network to emit coordinated population events.” Three groups do, in three substrates, independently. Learned event detection over a physiological time series is an established genre with a standard architecture family, and no claim about the architecture being new survives. Nor should one be made about the per-lab loop as an idea: CASCADE's stated central move is resampling its ground-truth database to match the noise level and sampling rate of the unseen recording, which is the same argument one level down, published in 2021.

What survives, taken from methods sections rather than from an absence of search results. Across the assembly literature the evaluation metric is membership, never event timing. Mölter scores a Best Match set-difference over cell groups. Russo and Durstewitz score a Rand index over unit assignment. Both plant or compute the occurrence times they decline to score. Two substrates, two independent groups, the same omission.

So the defensible claim is positional and narrow: nobody detects the coordinated event itself from per-cell calcium activity, against events planted in a simulation parameterised from the lab's own recordings. The substrate and the source of truth are what is new here — not the network.

3 · What the neighbours do better, measured

The most useful thing reading them produced was not a citation. The sleep-EEG detectors do not fix their scoring tolerance: DOSED reports F1 against an overlap criterion swept from 0.1 to 0.9, re-tuning every competitor at each value, and SEED adds mean overlap as a separate localization score. This project fixes its tolerance at 1.5 s and had never varied it. Varying it says two things at once.

The published ranking is safe. Whoever leads a regime leads it at every tolerance from 0.1 s to 3 s, so no comparison in this project rests on the choice. That is worth knowing rather than assuming.

But the tolerance sits past the point where the instrument stops reading. Every detector has plateaued by roughly three quarters of a second, and the median realized event footprint is 0.80 s — so the shipped tolerance is close to twice the width of the thing being found. Above the plateau the bench cannot distinguish a detector that lands on an event from one that lands a second away. Localization accuracy is not measured badly here; it is not measured. The detectors that move most across the sweep are the binned ones, which is exactly where timing error lives: CICADA reads 0.12 at the tightest tolerance and 0.56 at the shipped one, on the same detections.

5 · What this page does not establish

No method above has been run on this project's corpus. The comparison here is six detectors ported into this repo plus its own networks, and of those six only CICADA is published work — reaching us second-hand, as a port of a port. Until something from this page goes through the same fit-and-score procedure, “competes with state of the art” is not a supported sentence and does not appear in one.

A survey is not a proof of absence. This is twelve papers and two adjacent literatures, not a systematic review. Two of the four sub-questions in section 0 — the corpus and the comparison — were answerable only for the papers read in full, and two papers that bear directly on this are not on the shelf at all: Malvache et al. 2016, which is the primary source for the canonical SCE rule and is not open access, and SpikeNet, which is indexed but outside the open-access subset.

The claim that the field never scores event timing is about the papers read. It rests on two benchmarks — Mölter's and Russo and Durstewitz's — being explicit about their metrics. It would be overturned by a single assembly paper that reports temporal agreement, and no exhaustive search for one has been done.

Nothing here says any detector is right about a real slice. The corpus is simulated, every learned number is one training run per fold, and no seed error bars exist.
