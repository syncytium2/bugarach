1. The problem

{{PROSE:s1_open}}

Our lab films brain cells in thin slices of mouse brain. The cells are KNDy neurons, named for three chemicals they make (kisspeptin, neurokinin B and dynorphin). They sit in the hypothalamus, the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the reproductive system (Lehman et al. 2010). Each cell carries a dye that glows brighter when calcium enters it, which happens when the cell is active. A brief brightening is a calcium event.

A program run by the lab finds the events and writes down when each one happened. It sorts them into two lists, the fast stream and the slow stream: brief brightenings and much longer ones. The detectors in this document start from those lists of times; they never see the movie. We draw a list as a raster (Figure 1). Each row is one cell, and each tick is one event. When many cells are active at the same moment, their ticks line up in a vertical stripe. We call such a moment a coordinated event.

The lab wants to know whether drugs change how often these cells act together. That needs a program that marks coordinated events. We call such a program a detector, and each moment it marks a call. Two things make the job hard. First, cells have events on their own, so some ticks line up by accident, and the busier the cells, the more accidental lineups there are. Second, a real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which stripes are real. This matters for the lab's question. If a drug makes cells busier, a detector can report more or fewer coordinated events when coordination has not changed at all.

How this document goes. Because real recordings have no answer key, we also build simulated recordings in which we plant the coordinated events ourselves (Figure 1B). Every detector compares what it sees with what chance would give, so Section 2 starts with how chance is estimated. Sections 3 and 4 describe the detectors. Sections 5 and 6 describe the simulated recordings and how we grade a detector on them. Section 7 gives the scores. Section 8 shows the detectors on real recordings, where no score is possible. Section 10 says what the results mean for the lab's question.

2. Telling coordination from chance: surrogates

Suppose 6 cells each have an event in the same 2-second slice of time. Is that coordination? It depends on how often 6 cells line up by chance. One way to find out is to scramble the recording and count again. The scrambled copy is a surrogate. A good surrogate keeps each cell's own pattern of events and breaks only the timing between cells, so lineups that remain in it are chance lineups. A detector makes many surrogates and sets its bar, the count a real bin must pass, high among the surrogate counts. Often the bar is a percentile: the 99.9th percentile is the count that only 1 surrogate bin in 1,000 goes above.

There are two common ways to scramble:

  • Shuffle. Keep each cell's number of events, but move each event to a new random time.
  • Circular shift. Slide each cell's whole row of events left or right by its own random amount. Events pushed off the end wrap around to the start. Every gap between one cell's events stays the same, except where the row wraps.

Every detector in this project that uses surrogates uses the circular shift. Figure 2 shows why.

Why the shift and not the shuffle. A bar built from shuffles sits too low, so chance lineups pass it too often. Here is why. A detector counts each cell once per bin, however many events it has there. Real cells seldom have two events in one 2-second bin: in the fast stream, about {{sur_fast_doubles.real|.0f}} of every 1,000 events do. A circular shift keeps that ({{sur_fast_doubles.shift|.0f}}). A shuffle scatters events at random and raises it to about {{sur_fast_doubles.shuffle|.0f}} (Figure 2C). Each doubled event adds nothing to the count, so a shuffle makes lineups look rarer than they are. In Figure 2B1, the shuffle says 6 or more ROIs line up in {{sur_fast_n6.shuffle|.2%}} of bins; the shift says {{sur_fast_n6.shift|.2%}}. In the slow stream, cells never put two events in one bin, and a shuffle does so {{sur_slow_doubles.shuffle|.0f}} times per 1,000; the two lines sit closer. At 6 ROIs the shuffle sits below the shift in each of the four groups of mice (Section 8), in both streams. Others have measured the same failure for random scrambles and recommend shifting whole rows instead (Stella et al. 2022).

Real recordings line up more than a shift explains. At 8 or more ROIs the recordings sit {{sur_fast_real_over_shift.n8}} times above the shift in the fast stream, and at 10 or more about {{sur_fast_real_over_shift.n10}} times. That holds at 6 ROIs in each of the four groups. Part of this excess may be slow changes in activity that many cells share, which a shift of the whole baseline also breaks. The simulated recordings line up more than the shift too, because of their planted events, but less than the real ones at 8 or more ROIs (Figure 2B1).

Where the surrogate comes from matters too. In Figure 2D, the bar built from the whole recording is passed in {{sur_c.calls_global_in_block}} two-second bins inside the busy block, where nothing was planted. The bar built from the nearby 60 seconds rises with the block, to about {{sur_c.local_bar_in_block_median|.0f}} ROIs, and is passed {{sur_c.calls_local_in_block}} times. The cost is that a real event joined by fewer ROIs than that is also missed inside a busy stretch. Figure 14 measures that cost. In the minutes shown outside the block, each bar makes {{sur_c.outside_local}} calls: {{sur_c.outside_on_planted}} on a planted event and {{sur_c.outside_on_decoy}} on decoys; the two planted events it misses there are small ones, joined by 3 ROIs each. Judging chance from nearby time is an old idea in the study of spike timing (Harrison and Geman 2009; Amarasingham et al. 2012).

3.1 rate+context

It adds up every event from every ROI and asks how many arrived in about 1 second around each moment. It compares that with the average over the surrounding minute. Where the count beats the average by at least {{settings.rate.excess_threshold_hz|.0f}} events per second, it marks the moment. It joins marks less than 3 seconds apart into one call, and it drops a lone mark that lasts a single 0.1-second step.

What the design does well

Very fast and simple. The bar follows slow changes in how busy the recording is. It needs no surrogates.

What the design risks

It counts events, not cells, so one very busy cell can push it over the bar. The bar rises with the average but not with how much the rate jumps around, so busy stretches bring false alarms.

3.2 CoactDetect

It cuts time into {{settings.coact.int_win_sec|.0f}}-second bins and counts how many different ROIs have an event in each. A cell with three events in one bin still counts once. For each bin with at least 3 ROIs, it takes the {{settings.coact.context_win_sec|.0f}} seconds around that bin, makes {{settings.coact.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of that stretch, and counts again in each. It asks how far the real count sits above the surrogates' average, measured in typical spreads (standard deviations: how far surrogate counts usually stray from their average). At {{coact_bar.z}} spreads or more, the bin is marked. Marked bins less than 3 seconds apart become one call. For a smooth bell-shaped spread, that level is passed once in {{coact_bar.one_in}} bins by chance. Surrogate counts are rarely bell-shaped, so the true chance rate may differ; we have not measured it.

What the design does well

Counts cells, not events, so one bursting cell cannot fake a lineup. Its idea of chance comes from the nearby minute, so it makes few false alarms in busy stretches.

What the design risks

The same rising bar hides real events in busy stretches (Figure 14). The bin being tested sits inside the minute used to judge it, so a real event raises its own bar a little. Its bins sit at fixed times, so an event split across two bins can be missed.

3.3 LoCo

LoCo counts different ROIs per {{settings.loco.bin_width_sec|.0f}}-second bin. Every {{settings.loco.thr_step_sec|.0f}} seconds it sets a new bar. It takes the minute before that moment and the minute after, makes {{settings.loco.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of each, and finds the {{settings.loco.threshold_pctile}}th percentile of the surrogate counts on each side. The bar is the higher of the two, so it rises as soon as either side gets busy. A bin makes a call when its count passes the bar and at least 3 ROIs take part. Calls less than {{settings.loco.merge_gap_sec|.0f}} seconds apart are joined.

What the design does well

Counts cells, not events. The bar follows busy stretches, and taking the higher side raises it as soon as a busy stretch begins, so it makes few false alarms there.

What the design risks

The same rising bar hides real events in busy stretches (Figure 14). A second real event within a minute can raise the bar and hide the first; the test recordings keep planted events 2 minutes apart, so this is not measured here. Its bins sit at fixed times.

3.4 binned SCE

This detector counts different ROIs per {{settings.sce.bin_width_sec|.0f}}-second bin. It makes {{settings.sce.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of the whole stretch it is analyzing: the whole simulated recording in Figure 6, and each marked window on real recordings. It pools every surrogate count from every bin and sets one bar at the {{settings.sce.threshold_pctile|.0f}}th percentile. Any bin whose count is above the bar, with at least 3 ROIs, is a call.

What the design does well

Simple, and close to a rule used for more than twenty years. One bar per stretch is easy to explain and check. Wide bins still catch events whose cells start a few seconds apart.

What the design risks

One bar cannot follow busy stretches, so it calls all through them. The busy stretch also sits inside its surrogates and may raise its bar for the rest of the recording (not tested). Wide bins mix a short event with many seconds of background.

3.5 locust

locust turns each event into a short "on" period: its ROI counts as switched on for {{settings.cicada.active_duration_sec|.0f}} second. It then counts how many ROIs are on in each 0.1-second frame. For the bar, it slides each ROI's whole row by a random number of frames, {{settings.cicada.n_surrogates}} times, pools all the frame counts, and takes the {{settings.cicada.sce_percentile}}th percentile. The bar is the same for the whole recording, including any drug periods. It calls each peak of the count that reaches the bar, keeping peaks at least 4 frames (0.4 seconds) apart. Unlike three of the others, it has no minimum number of ROIs.

All six hand-written detectors place each event at the same moment: when its brightening reached half its full rise.

locust is adapted from CICADA, software released by the Cossart lab (Section 11). It differs from CICADA in four ways: we feed it our own list of events instead of letting CICADA find them; each event is on for a fixed 1 second; its percentile was re-chosen by this project; and it uses one bar for the whole recording. Its results are its own. Nobody has compared them with CICADA's.

What the design does well

Works at the time step of one frame. Counts cells, not events. Close to openly released software from another lab.

What the design risks

One bar for the whole recording, so busy stretches bring many false alarms. The on-period length is fixed by hand, and a different length gives different results.

3.6 SPIKE-synch

SPIKE-synch gives every event a score from 0 to 1: the share of the other ROIs that have an event close to it. To decide whether two events in different ROIs are close, it looks at four gaps. For each of the two events, it measures the time back to the previous event in the same ROI and forward to the next one. The two events are close if they are nearer than half the shortest of those four gaps, and never more than {{settings.sync.tau_max}} seconds apart. A busy cell has short gaps, so it gets a tight window and cannot line up with everything just by being busy.

The detector then takes the average score of the events in each 0.1-second frame. A call starts when a frame's score passes {{settings.sync.C_threshold}} and continues while later frames stay above {{settings.sync.C_min}}; a gap shorter than {{settings.sync.max_gap}} seconds does not end it. A call must include at least 3 events. The {{settings.sync.tau_max}}-second cap and the {{settings.sync.C_threshold}} level are this project's choices, not the measure's authors'.

Those choices set a hard limit. An event joined by 10% of {{bench_n_roi}} ROIs has 3 cells, so each of its events can score at most 2 out of {{a_event.others}}, about 0.06. That never passes {{settings.sync.C_threshold}}, so SPIKE-synch at its shipped setting cannot call such an event at all.

What the design does well

Needs no surrogates. Its gap-based window keeps busy cells from lining up with everything.

What the design risks

The score is a share of all ROIs, so at the shipped setting it cannot call an event joined by few cells. The {{settings.sync.tau_max}}-second cap is tighter than the spread of many planted events.

4. The learned detectors

A learned detector is a small neural network: a chain of simple arithmetic steps, each with numbers the computer can adjust. The computer shows it many examples and adjusts the numbers until the output is high inside planted events and low everywhere else. That adjusting is training. In training, the busy block and the decoys are marked as "no event", so the networks are taught directly not to call them. The network then picks its call level, the output a moment must reach to become a call.

There are two sets of copies. The copies drawn in Figure 9 and run on real recordings in Section 8 trained on {{learned_meta.tube.n_fit}} simulated recordings and picked their call level on {{learned_meta.tube.n_val}} more. The copies graded in Section 7 were trained fresh in each round (Section 6.3). Each tube model has {{learned_meta.tube.n_params|,}} adjustable numbers. All of them see the same input: the raster cut into 0.1-second frames, with a 1 wherever a cell has an event.

4.1 tube, and three variations

The name comes from a picture: look down a dark tube as the recording slides past, and a coordinated event is a bright flash.

  1. It widens each event slightly (to {{tube_widen_s}} seconds here; the width is learned). Then, frame by frame, it takes the share of ROIs that are on. This gives one line: the "brightness" of the recording over time.
  2. It subtracts the brightness over the surrounding seconds (the surround) from the brightness right now (the center). It does this with filters: weighted averages over nearby moments, one narrow for the center and one wide for the surround, in four pairs (Figure 9A). The widths are learned. For tube, the center filters are about {{tube_sigma.centre.0}} to {{tube_sigma.centre.3}} seconds wide and the surround filters about {{tube_sigma.surround.0}} to {{tube_sigma.surround.3}} seconds wide. No filter reaches more than {{tube_sigma.cutoff_s}} seconds before or after the moment it scores, so the widest surround is nearly flat.
  3. A small network combines those comparisons, and the brightness itself, into a score from 0 to 1. Where the score reaches the call level, it marks the moment; marks less than 2 seconds apart become one call.

Center against surround is the same idea as "now" against "nearby" in the hand-written detectors, except the computer picks the widths. The variations:

  • tube-guard blanks the surround within 0.8 seconds of the moment scored, so an event raises its own bar less. Radar detectors do the same with "guard cells".
  • tube-ratio divides the center by the surround instead of subtracting. It is meant to make a background twice as busy need a flash twice as bright. The raw brightness also reaches the network, so that is not guaranteed.
  • tube-ratio-guard makes both changes.

4.2 trace and tiny

We also trained two simpler networks as controls, to check whether tube's design is what makes it work. trace skips the center and surround and looks at the share of ROIs on, plus the number of ROIs. tiny filters each cell's row on its own before combining them. Both have more adjustable numbers than tube, and they were trained at one tenth of tube's learning rate (the size of each adjustment). Neither learned to mark events: on the test recording, tiny made one call that covered all of it, and trace made {{trace_bench_seed_score.calls}} calls that together covered {{trace_bench_seed_score.time_covered|pct}} of it. Because their training differed, their failure does not show that tube's design is what makes it work.

What the design does well

Learns its own time scales instead of taking them from a person. Once trained it scans a recording thousands of times faster than real time. The ratio versions make almost no false alarms in busy stretches.

What the design risks

It knows only what simulated recordings taught it, and it was taught on the very busy block it is tested against. The ratio versions miss every real event inside a busy stretch (Figure 14). It reports a moment, not which cells took part. The call level is picked on 2 simulated recordings and used unchanged on real ones.

Why each layer is there. The busy block tests whether a detector calls just because cells are busy. Decoys stand for lineups a careful person would not count, so a detector cannot score well just by calling every lineup. A detector that sees only event times cannot tell a decoy from a planted event joined by 18% of ROIs. So even one that called every lineup perfectly would have at most {{bench.n_planted}} correct calls out of {{bench.n_planted}} plus {{bench.n_distractors}}: a precision of {{bench.precision_ceiling}} and an F1 score of {{f1_ceiling|.2f}} (Section 6.1). Planted events are kept {{bench.min_sep_s|.0f}} seconds apart so the nearby window of CoactDetect, LoCo and rate+context never holds two of them. That spacing avoids a known weakness of those detectors rather than testing it (Section 10). Decoys follow no spacing rule and can sit close to a planted event.

Two background levels. Each random starting number (a seed) gives a different recording from the same recipe. We run the recipe at two background levels: quiet, {{bench.quiet_mhz}} mHz per ROI on average, and busy, {{bench.busy_mhz|.0f}} mHz. The project read both from untreated baselines only, never from drug periods. Recomputed on the {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.n}} baselines the simulator was fitted to, a recording's average ROI rate has its 25th percentile at {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.p25}} mHz and its 75th at {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.p75}} mHz, matching the two levels. Counting planted events and decoys too, the simulated recordings carry {{bench_realized_mhz.quiet}} and {{bench_realized_mhz.busy}} mHz outside their busy block. The busy block is not a third level: it appears in every recording at both levels.

What the simulator gets wrong.

  • Its busiest cells bunch far too much. For ROIs below 50 mHz, the simulated bunching matches real recordings ({{gen_fitted_fano_by_rate.under_50.w30}} against {{gen_real_fano_by_rate.under_50.w30}} in 30-second windows). For ROIs above 100 mHz, real cells are close to unbunched ({{gen_real_fano_by_rate.over_100.w30}}), while simulated ones read {{gen_fitted_fano_by_rate.over_100.w30}}. Busy cells make most chance lineups, so this matters.
  • Its timing spread is not well measured. The {{bench.jitter_s}}-second spread of planted events came from lineups in real recordings. Lineups under a circular shift, which are chance lineups, measured the same way give 0.42 seconds. And a recording built at 0.36 seconds measures back as about 0.64 seconds. ⚠ Not resolved.
  • Its lineups are rarer than real ones at 8 or more ROIs (Figure 2B1).
  • It makes no recording faults, and every planted event has the same kind of timing spread.

A score on simulated recordings does not promise the same score on real ones.

6. How we grade and tune a detector

6.1 Grading a call

A call is a hit if it covers a planted event's time or sits within {{tol_s}} seconds of it. Pairs of calls and planted events are matched closest first, and each call can claim only one event. A planted event with no call is a miss. Any other call is a false alarm, including a second call on an event already claimed and a call on a decoy. From these we get three scores, all from 0 to 1:

  • Recall: the share of planted events found.
  • Precision: the share of calls that were hits. Calls in the busy block are left out of precision and counted on their own (Section 6.2).
  • F1 score: one number that is high only when both recall and precision are high.

We also report recall separately for events joined by {{bench.participation_pct.0}}%, {{bench.participation_pct.1}}% and {{bench.participation_pct.2}}% of ROIs, because a detector that finds only large events is a different tool from one that also finds small ones. An event joined by 10% of ROIs has 3 cells, exactly the minimum three of the detectors require.

The {{tol_s}}-second window was chosen where LoCo's and CoactDetect's F1 scores stop rising as the window widens. It is generous: a planted event's cells usually start within about 1 second. A wide window rewards wide calls, such as binned SCE's 10-second bins. Read the order of the scores, not their last decimal place.

6.2 A limit on calls where nothing was planted

Because busy-block calls are left out of precision, F1 cannot see them. So each hand-written detector has a limit on calls per minute in the busy block: {{ceilings.coact|g}} for CoactDetect, LoCo and SPIKE-synch, {{ceilings.rate|g}} for rate+context, {{ceilings.sce|g}} for binned SCE and {{ceilings.cicada|g}} for locust. Each limit was set above that detector's own earlier measured rate. So a limit flags a detector that has drifted worse than its own past; it does not compare detectors. The learned detectors have no limit yet.

The limit tells you nothing about the opposite mistake: a detector can make no calls in the busy block because it cannot find anything there. Figure 14 measures that separately.

6.3 Choosing a setting without cheating

Each hand-written detector has one main setting that raises or lowers its bar, and we try every value on a list. A fair test must not choose the setting on the recordings it is scored on. We made {{rounds.n_recordings}} bench recordings and split them into {{rounds.n_folds}} groups of {{rounds.per_fold}} (Figure 11B). In each of {{rounds.n_folds}} rounds, the setting with the best F1 on the other {{rounds.n_choose}} recordings is scored on the {{rounds.per_fold}} it never saw. When two values tie, the looser one wins. In each round, each learned detector is trained three times from different random starting numbers, on 12 of those {{rounds.n_choose}} recordings: 10 to train and 2 to pick its call level. In one of the three training runs the random start also picked a different set of training recordings.

The rounds choose by F1 alone. The project also has a stricter rule that refuses a setting at the end of its list, a setting from a list where every value scores the same, and a setting that breaks the busy-block limit. We did not use that rule here, so Section 7 reports some settings it would refuse.

We also score every hand-written detector at the setting it ships with, which Figures 3 to 8 and the real recordings use. Those settings were not chosen on these 24 recordings, but they are not independent of the bench either. locust's percentile was re-chosen on these two background levels; CoactDetect's and SPIKE-synch's came from the project's viewer. They are provisional.

Read this first. A real recording has no answer key. These figures show where each detector made calls. They cannot show whether a call is right, and nothing here measures accuracy or compares a drug period with baseline.

  • rate+context, CoactDetect, SPIKE-synch and the learned detectors run on each marked window separately.
  • binned SCE runs on each marked window, with one bar per window.
  • LoCo scans the whole recording, taking its surrogates within each period.
  • locust scans the whole recording, with one bar for all of it.

10. What this means, and what could be wrong

What this means for the lab's question. {{PROSE:s10_meaning_real}} On simulated recordings, no detector is shown to be best. And every design either calls through busy stretches or goes partly blind in them. So a detector's calls cannot, on their own, show that a drug changed coordination: a drug that makes cells busier changes the calls too. Any such study needs a way to separate the two, and none of these detectors provides one yet.

What could be wrong with the conclusions above. Each item names the conclusion it threatens.

  • All accuracy numbers come from simulated recordings (Section 7). Their busiest cells bunch far more than real ones, their lineups are rarer at large sizes, and their timing spread is poorly measured (Section 5).
  • The scoring window is generous and was chosen where the leading detectors stop improving (Section 6.1). It favors wide calls and may flatter the leaders.
  • Decoys cannot be told apart from planted events, which caps F1 at {{f1_ceiling|.2f}} and makes most false alarms decoy calls (Section 7).
  • Planted events are always 2 minutes apart, which hides a known weakness of the detectors that judge chance locally: a second event nearby can raise the bar (Sections 3.3, 5). The project has a crowded test recording for this; it was not run here.
  • No statistical test backs "about the same" or "lower" (Section 7).
  • The learned detectors were taught against the same busy block and decoys they are scored on, so their busy-block results may not carry over to other kinds of busy stretch (Sections 4, 7).
  • SPIKE-synch's cap and level are this project's choices, and they decide what it can find (Section 3.6).
  • The busy-stretch test (Figure 14) uses one kind of busy stretch: extra random events in every cell. Real busy stretches may look different.
  • The shipped settings are provisional, some were tuned on this recipe, and the fast-stream settings are used unchecked on the slow stream (Sections 6.3, 8).
  • The real-data figures show one recording per group per drug, chosen by size, with no person checking the calls, and agreement between detectors was not measured (Section 8).
  • The bake-off numbers are provisional until the project releases them.

What this document leaves out.

  • Whether a drug changes coordination. Nothing here compares a drug period with baseline.
  • Methods that learn without an answer key. The lab is building detectors that learn from real recordings with no planted events; they are not ready.
  • A person's review of the calls, which is how the lab uses these detectors.

11. Where the methods come from

  • rate+context has the structure of radar's averaging detector, which compares each point with the average of its neighbors; rate+context adds a fixed amount to that average where radar multiplies it. Finn H.M., Johnson R.S. (1968). Adaptive detection mode with threshold control as a function of spatially sampled clutter-level estimates. RCA Review 29(3):414–464. We have not searched the neuroscience literature on thresholding a population's firing rate.

Detection and significance. Neural Computation 14(1):43–80, doi:10.1162/089976602753284455; and II. Nonstationary data, 14(1):81–119, doi:10.1162/089976602753284464, which adds a moving window.
  • Judging chance from nearby time: Harrison M.T., Geman S. (2009). A rate and history-preserving resampling algorithm for neural spike trains. Neural Computation 21(5):1244–1258, doi:10.1162/neco.2008.03-08-730. Amarasingham A., Harrison M.T., Hatsopoulos N.G., Geman S. (2012). Conditional modeling and the jitter method of spike resampling. J Neurophysiol 107:517–531, doi:10.1152/jn.00633.2011.
  • Shifting whole rows of events as surrogates: Pipa G., Wheeler D.W., Singer W., Nikolić D. (2008). NeuroXidence: reliable and efficient analysis of an excess or deficiency of joint-spike events. J Comput Neurosci 25:64–88, doi:10.1007/s10827-007-0065-3 (small shifts, without wrapping around). The wrap-around form for calcium events: Bocchio M. et al. The measure's authors pick out events on the same scores, per event rather than per frame and together with a condition on the calcium signal itself (Cecchini G. et al. 2021, PLoS Comput Biol 17:e1008963, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008963; T. 12. Word list

Calcium event
A brief brightening of one cell in the movie, which means the cell was active.
Coordinated event
A moment when many cells are active together.
ROI (region of interest)
The patch of the movie treated as one cell. We use "ROI" and "cell" for the same thing.
Fast stream, slow stream
The lab's event-finding program keeps brief brightenings and much longer ones in two separate lists. We analyze each on its own.
Raster
A picture of a recording with one row per cell and one tick per event.
Lane
A strip above a raster that marks one detector's calls, or the planted events.
Detector, call
A program that marks coordinated events; each moment it marks is a call. A call is a claim, not a fact.
Lineup
Several cells with events at about the same time, whether by coordination or by chance.
Rate, mHz
How often a cell has events, in millihertz: 1 mHz is one event every 1,000 seconds.
Bin, frame
A bin is a short, fixed slice of time, such as 2 seconds. A frame is one picture of the movie, 0.1 seconds here.
Surrogate, circular shift, shuffle
A surrogate is a scrambled copy of a recording used to see what chance gives. A circular shift slides each cell's whole row; a shuffle moves each event to a random time.
Bar
The value a detector's measure must pass before it makes a call.
Percentile, median
The 99th percentile of a list is the value that 99% of the list falls below. The median is the 50th percentile, the middle value.
Typical spread, variance
How far values usually stray from their average (standard deviation); variance is that spread squared.
Log scale
An axis on which each step is ten times the one before, so very different sizes fit on one plot.
Simulator, bench, seed
The simulator writes made-up recordings with a known answer; the bench is its standard recipe; a seed is the random starting number that makes one recording.
Planted event, decoy
A planted event is a coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose. A decoy is built the same way but left out of the answer key.
Quiet level, busy level
The two background rates the bench runs at: {{bench.quiet_mhz}} and {{bench.busy_mhz|.0f}} mHz per ROI.
Busy block
Five minutes inside every bench recording, at both levels, where every ROI gets extra random events and nothing is planted.
Hit, miss, false alarm
A hit is a call on a planted event; a miss is a planted event with no call; a false alarm is any other call.
Recall, precision, F1 score
Recall is the share of planted events found; precision is the share of calls that were hits; F1 is one score that is high only when both are.
Setting, shipped setting, round
A setting is a number that controls a detector, such as how high its bar sits. The shipped setting is the one it comes with. A round is one pass of choosing a setting on some recordings and scoring it on others.
Neural network, training, learning rate, call level
A learned detector is a chain of arithmetic steps whose numbers the computer adjusts (training), by steps of a chosen size (learning rate). The call level is the output a moment must reach to become a call.
Filter, center, surround
A filter is a weighted average over nearby moments. The center filter looks at the moment scored; the surround filter looks at the seconds around it.
Control
A deliberately simpler version, used to check whether a design choice matters.
Baseline, TTX, senktide, high K+, marked windows
Baseline is the untreated start of a recording. TTX blocks fast electrical spikes; senktide switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor; high K+ makes healthy cells active. Marked windows are the stretches of each period the lab analyzes.
DI, MALE, ORX, OVX
The four groups of mice: females in diestrus, males, males with testes removed, and females with ovaries removed.
SCE
Synchronous calcium event, the name a long-used method gives to a coordinated event.
