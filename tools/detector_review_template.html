Coordinated-event detectors

Computer programs that look for moments when many brain cells become active together: how each one decides, how we tested them, and what they report on real recordings.

1. The problem

{{PROSE:s1_open}}

Our lab films brain cells in thin slices of mouse brain. The cells are KNDy neurons, named for three chemicals they make (kisspeptin, neurokinin B and dynorphin). They sit in the hypothalamus, the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the reproductive system (Lehman et al. 2010). Each cell carries a dye that glows brighter when calcium enters it, which happens when the cell is active. A brief brightening is a calcium event. We treat each patch of the movie that holds one cell as a region of interest (ROI); in this document "ROI" and "cell" mean the same thing.

A program run by the lab finds the events and writes down each one's time: the moment its brightening reached half its full height. It keeps two lists, the fast stream and the slow stream, for brief brightenings and much longer ones. The detectors start from those lists of times; they never see the movie. We draw a list as a raster: one row per cell, one tick per event. When many cells are active at the same moment, their ticks line up in a vertical stripe, a coordinated event.

Each recording starts with an untreated baseline, then a drug, and often a final period of high potassium (high K+) that makes healthy cells active. The mice come from four groups: DI (females in diestrus, one stage of the reproductive cycle), MALE (males), ORX (males with testes removed) and OVX (females with ovaries removed).

The lab wants to know whether drugs change how often these cells act together. That needs a detector. Two things make the job hard. First, cells have events on their own, so some ticks line up by accident, and the busier the cells, the more accidental lineups there are. So if a drug makes cells busier, a detector can report more or fewer coordinated events when coordination has not changed. Second, a real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which stripes are real. (The drug TTX does not give one either; Section 8 explains why.) So we also build simulated recordings in which we plant the coordinated events ourselves (Figure 1B) and grade the detectors there.

2. Telling coordination from chance: surrogates

Suppose 6 cells each have an event in the same 2-second bin (a short, fixed slice of time). Is that coordination? It depends on how often 6 cells line up by chance. One way to find out is to scramble the recording and count again. The scrambled copy is a surrogate. A good surrogate keeps each cell's own pattern of events and breaks only the timing between cells, so lineups left in it are chance lineups. A detector makes many surrogates, counts lineups in each, and sets its bar near the top of those counts; a real bin must pass the bar to be called. Often the bar is a percentile: the 99.9th percentile is the count that only 1 surrogate bin in 1,000 goes above.

There are two common ways to scramble:

  • Shuffle. Keep each cell's number of events, but move each event to a new random time.
  • Circular shift. Slide each cell's whole row of events left or right by its own random amount. Events pushed off the end wrap around to the start. Every gap between one cell's events stays the same, except where the row wraps.

Every detector in this project that uses surrogates uses the circular shift. Figure 2 shows why.

Why the shift and not the shuffle. A bar built from shuffles sits too low, so chance lineups pass it too often. The chain goes like this. A detector counts each cell once per bin. A shuffle drops more of a cell's events into bins that cell already filled: in the fast stream, {{sur_fast_doubles.shuffle|.1f}} of every 1,000 events, against {{sur_fast_doubles.real|.1f}} in the recording and {{sur_fast_doubles.shift|.1f}} after a shift (Figure 2C). Those extra events add nothing to the count, so the shuffled copies have fewer lineups, the bar comes out lower, and chance lineups in the real recording pass it more easily. In Figure 2B1, the shuffle says 6 or more cells line up in {{sur_fast_n6.shuffle|.2%}} of bins; the shift says {{sur_fast_n6.shift|.2%}}. At 6 cells the shuffle sits below the shift in every group of mice, in both streams. Stella et al. (2022) found a related failure when each spike is moved on its own, and recommend shifting whole trains instead.

Real recordings line up more than a shift explains. At 8 or more cells the recordings sit {{sur_fast_real_over_shift.n8}} times above the shift in the fast stream, and at 10 or more about {{sur_fast_real_over_shift.n10}} times. Part of this may be slow changes in activity that many cells share, which shifting a whole baseline also breaks.

Where the surrogate comes from matters too. In the sketch of Figure 2D, the bar built from the whole recording is passed in {{sur_c.calls_global_in_block}} two-second bins inside the busy block, where nothing was planted. The bar built from the nearby 60 seconds rises with the block, to about {{sur_c.local_bar_in_block_median|.0f}} cells, and is passed {{sur_c.calls_local_in_block}} times. The cost is that a real event joined by fewer cells than that is also missed inside a busy stretch; Figure 13 measures that cost for the real detectors. Judging chance from nearby time is an old idea in the study of nerve-cell spikes (the brief electrical signals nerve cells send): Date et al. 1998; Harrison and Geman 2009; Amarasingham et al. 2012.

3. The six hand-written detectors

A hand-written detector follows fixed rules a person wrote. All six ask one question: are more cells lining up right now than chance explains? They split three ways on where chance comes from, and that split decides how they behave when cells get busy (Section 7):

  • From the nearby seconds: rate+context, CoactDetect and LoCo.
  • From the whole stretch being analyzed: binned SCE and locust.
  • No surrogates at all: SPIKE-synch.

3.1 rate+context

It adds up every event from every cell and asks how many arrived in about 1 second around each moment. It compares that with the average over the surrounding minute. Where the count beats the average by at least {{settings.rate.excess_threshold_hz|.0f}} events per second, it flags the moment. It joins flags less than 3 seconds apart into one call and drops a lone flag that lasts a single 0.1-second step.

What the design does well

Very fast and simple. The bar follows slow changes in how busy the recording is. It needs no surrogates.

What the design risks

It counts events, not cells, so one very busy cell can push it over the bar. The bar rises with the average but not with how much the rate jumps around, so very busy stretches bring false alarms.

3.2 CoactDetect

It cuts time into {{settings.coact.int_win_sec|.0f}}-second bins and counts how many different cells have an event in each; a cell with three events in one bin still counts once. For each bin with at least 3 cells, it takes the {{settings.coact.context_win_sec|.0f}} seconds around the bin, makes {{settings.coact.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of that stretch, and counts again in each. It then asks how far the real count sits above the surrogates' average. It measures that distance in typical spreads: how far surrogate counts usually stray from their average (the standard deviation). At {{coact_bar.z}} typical spreads or more, the bin is flagged; flagged bins less than 3 seconds apart become one call. If surrogate counts formed a smooth bell curve, chance alone would pass that bar in about 1 tested bin in {{coact_bar.one_in}}. Surrogate counts are small and lopsided, so the real chance rate is higher than that; we have not measured it.

What the design does well

Counts cells, not events, so one bursting cell cannot fake a lineup. Its idea of chance comes from the nearby minute, so it makes few false alarms in busy stretches.

What the design risks

The same rising bar can hide real events in busy stretches (measured in Figure 13). The bin being tested sits inside the minute used to judge it, so a real event may raise its own bar a little (not measured). Its bins sit at fixed times, so an event split across two bins can be missed.

3.3 LoCo

LoCo counts different cells per {{settings.loco.bin_width_sec|.0f}}-second bin. Every {{settings.loco.thr_step_sec|.0f}} seconds it sets a new bar. It takes the minute before that moment and the minute after, makes {{settings.loco.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of each, and finds the {{settings.loco.threshold_pctile}}th percentile of the surrogate counts on each side. The bar is the higher of the two, so it rises as soon as either side gets busy. A bin makes a call when its count passes the bar and at least 3 cells take part; calls less than {{settings.loco.merge_gap_sec|.0f}} seconds apart are joined.

What the design does well

Counts cells, not events. The bar follows busy stretches, and taking the higher side raises it as soon as a busy stretch begins, so it makes few false alarms there.

What the design risks

The same rising bar can hide real events in busy stretches (Figure 13). Two real events less than a minute apart can raise each other's bar; the test recordings keep planted events 2 minutes apart, so this is not measured here. Its bins sit at fixed times.

3.4 binned SCE

This detector counts different cells per {{settings.sce.bin_width_sec|.0f}}-second bin. It makes {{settings.sce.n_surrogates}} circular shifts of the whole stretch it is analyzing: the whole simulated recording in Figure 6, and each marked window on real recordings. It pools every surrogate count from every bin and sets one bar at the {{settings.sce.threshold_pctile|.0f}}th percentile. Any bin whose count is above the bar, with at least 3 cells, is a call; neighboring called bins are not joined.

What the design does well

Simple, and close to a rule used for more than twenty years. One bar per stretch is easy to explain and check. Wide bins still catch events whose cells start a few seconds apart.

What the design risks

One bar cannot follow busy stretches, so it calls all through them. The busy stretch also sits inside its surrogates and may raise its bar for the rest of the stretch (not tested). Wide bins mix a short event with many seconds of background.

3.5 locust

locust turns each event into a short "on" period: its cell counts as switched on for {{settings.cicada.active_duration_sec|.0f}} second. It then counts how many cells are on in each 0.1-second frame. For the bar, it slides each cell's whole row by a random number of frames, {{settings.cicada.n_surrogates}} times, pools all the frame counts, and takes the {{settings.cicada.sce_percentile}}th percentile. The bar is the same for the whole recording, including any drug periods; the software it came from does the same. It calls each peak of the count that reaches the bar, keeping peaks at least 4 frames (0.4 seconds) apart. It has no minimum number of cells.

locust is adapted from CICADA, software released by the Cossart lab (Section 11). It differs from the version of CICADA we ported in three ways: we feed it the lab's list of events instead of letting CICADA find events itself; each event is on for a fixed 1 second; and its percentile was re-chosen by this project. The current CICADA software can also start from lists of event times; we have not compared locust with it. An earlier version of this port, in the lab's MATLAB code, was set aside for calling too often on the slow stream's long events. locust's results are its own; nobody has compared them with CICADA's.

What the design does well

Works at the time step of one frame. Counts cells, not events. Close to openly released software from another lab.

What the design risks

One bar for the whole recording, so busy stretches bring many false alarms. A version that sets one bar per period exists in the code but has not been tested. The on-period length is fixed by hand.

3.6 SPIKE-synch

SPIKE-synch gives every event a score from 0 to 1: the share of the other cells that have an event close to it. To decide whether two events in different cells are close, it looks at four gaps: for each of the two events, the time back to the previous event in the same cell and forward to the next one. The two events count as close only if they are less than half the shortest of those gaps apart, and also less than {{settings.sync.tau_max}} seconds apart. A busy cell has short gaps, so it gets a tight limit and cannot line up with everything just by being busy.

The detector then scores each 0.1-second frame, roughly as the average score of the events in it. A call starts when a frame's score passes {{settings.sync.C_threshold}} and continues while later frames stay above {{settings.sync.C_min}}; a gap shorter than {{settings.sync.max_gap}} seconds does not end it. A call must include at least 3 events. The {{settings.sync.tau_max}}-second limit and the {{settings.sync.C_threshold}} bar are this project's choices, not the measure's authors'.

Those choices set a hard limit. An event joined by 10% of {{bench_n_roi}} cells has 3 cells, so each of its events can score at most 2 out of {{a_event.others}}, about 0.06, unless background events happen to join it. That is below {{settings.sync.C_threshold}}, so SPIKE-synch at its shipped setting almost never calls such an event.

What the design does well

Needs no surrogates. Its gap-based limit keeps busy cells from lining up with everything.

What the design risks

The score is a share of all cells, so at the shipped setting it misses events joined by few cells. The {{settings.sync.tau_max}}-second limit is tighter than the spread of many planted events. When every cell is busy, chance alone lifts scores toward its bar (Section 7).

4. The learned detectors

A learned detector is a small neural network: a chain of simple arithmetic steps, each with numbers the computer can adjust. The computer shows it many simulated examples and adjusts the numbers until its output is high inside planted events and low everywhere else. That adjusting is training. In training, we label the busy block and the decoys "no event", so the networks are taught not to call them, and never see a real event inside a busy stretch. We then set its call level, the output a moment must reach to become a call, on recordings it did not train on.

We trained the learned detectors in two ways. The version drawn in Figure 9, also run on real recordings in Section 8, trained on {{learned_meta.tube.n_fit}} simulated recordings and set its call level on {{learned_meta.tube.n_val}} more. The versions graded in Section 7 were trained fresh in each round (Section 6.3). All of them see the raster cut into 0.1-second frames, with a 1 wherever a cell has an event.

4.1 tube, and three variations

The name comes from a picture: look down a dark tube as the recording slides past, and a coordinated event is a bright flash. Each tube model has {{learned_meta.tube.n_params|,}} adjustable numbers.

  1. It stretches each event into a short on-period (training set it to {{tube_widen_s}} seconds for tube). Then, frame by frame, it takes the share of cells that are on. This gives one line: the "brightness" of the recording over time.
  2. It subtracts the brightness over the surrounding seconds (the surround) from the brightness right now (the center). It does this with filters, weighted averages over nearby moments: a narrow one for the center and a broad one for the surround, in four pairs (Figure 9A). The widths are learned. For tube, the center filters have a spread (standard deviation) of about {{tube_sigma.centre.0}} to {{tube_sigma.centre.3}} seconds and the surround filters about {{tube_sigma.surround.0}} to {{tube_sigma.surround.3}} seconds, cut off {{tube_sigma.cutoff_s}} seconds before and after the moment scored.
  3. A small network combines those comparisons, and the brightness itself, into a score from 0 to 1, looking up to about 19 seconds either side. Where the score reaches the call level, it flags the moment; flags less than 2 seconds apart become one call.

The variations:

  • tube-guard blanks the surround within 0.8 seconds of the moment scored, so an event lifts its own surround less. Radar engineers use the same trick.
  • tube-ratio divides the center by the surround instead of subtracting. The idea: if the background doubles, a flash must double too before it is called. The network also sees the raw brightness, so the model need not follow that idea.
  • tube-ratio-guard makes both changes.

We also trained two simpler networks, trace and tiny, as checks on whether tube's design matters. Neither learned to mark events (on the test recording, tiny made one call covering all of it, and trace made {{trace_bench_seed_score.calls}} covering {{trace_bench_seed_score.time_covered|pct}}). They trained with a smaller learning rate than tube, so their failure proves nothing about tube's design. We leave them out of most figures.

What the design does well

Learns its own time scales instead of taking them from a person. The ratio versions make almost no false alarms in busy stretches.

What the design risks

It knows only what simulated recordings taught it, and it was taught that busy stretches hold no events. It reports a moment, not which cells took part. The call level is set on 2 simulated recordings and used unchanged on real ones.

The busy block tests whether a detector calls just because cells are busy. It appears in every recording, on both backgrounds below. It adds far more activity than most real drug periods (Section 8).

Decoys are lineups we leave off the answer key on purpose. They stand in for lineups that look real but that a careful person would not count, so a detector cannot score well just by calling every lineup. A detector that sees only event times cannot tell a decoy from a planted event joined by 18% of cells, so even one that called every lineup perfectly would score an F1 of only about {{f1_ceiling|.2f}} (Section 6.1). Decoys follow no spacing rule and can sit close to a planted event.

Spacing. Planted events are kept {{bench.min_sep_s|.0f}} seconds apart, so the nearby window of CoactDetect, LoCo and rate+context never holds two of them. That avoids a known weakness of those detectors rather than testing it (Section 10).

Two backgrounds. Each random starting number (a seed) gives a different recording from the same recipe. We run it with two background rates: quiet, set to {{bench.quiet_mhz}} mHz per cell on average, and busy, set to {{bench.busy_mhz|.0f}} mHz. The project read both from untreated baselines only. On the {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.n}} baselines in the current recordings that pass the simulator-fitting program's rules, a recording's average rate per cell has its 25th percentile at {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.p25}} mHz and its 75th at {{gen_real_rate_percentiles_mhz.p75}} mHz, close to the two settings. The simulator's background comes out a little below its settings ({{bench_realized_mhz.quiet_background_only}} and {{bench_realized_mhz.busy_background_only}} mHz); with planted events and decoys added, the recordings average {{bench_realized_mhz.quiet}} and {{bench_realized_mhz.busy}} mHz outside the busy block.

What the simulator gets wrong.

  • Its busiest cells bunch far too much (Figure 10E). In 30-second stretches, cells below 50 mHz match real ones ({{gen_fitted_fano_by_rate.under_50.w30}} against {{gen_real_fano_by_rate.under_50.w30}}). Cells above 100 mHz are close to unbunched in real recordings ({{gen_real_fano_by_rate.over_100.w30}}) but read {{gen_fitted_fano_by_rate.over_100.w30}} in the simulator. The simulator multiplies each cell's rate by random swings of the same relative size, so its bunching grows with rate. Busy cells make most chance lineups, so this matters.
  • Its timing scatter is not well measured. The {{bench.jitter_s}}-second scatter came from measuring lineups in real recordings. The same measurement gives 0.42 seconds on chance-only lineups and 0.64 seconds on a simulated recording built at 0.36 seconds. It does not return what went in, so real events may be tighter or looser than the planted ones. ⚠ Not resolved.
  • Its lineups are rarer than real ones at every size (Figure 2B1).
  • Every recording has {{bench.n_roi}} cells. Several detectors' rules depend on the number of cells, and real recordings range from about half to more than that.
  • It makes no recording faults, and every planted event has the same kind of timing scatter.

A score on simulated recordings does not promise the same score on real ones.

6. How we grade and tune a detector

6.1 Grading a call

A call is a hit if it covers a planted event's time or sits within {{tol_s}} seconds of it. We match calls to planted events closest pairs first, and each call can claim only one event. A planted event with no call is a miss. Any other call is a false alarm, including a second call on an event already claimed and a call on a decoy. From these we get three scores, all from 0 to 1:

  • Recall: the share of planted events found.
  • Precision: the share of calls that were hits. Calls in the busy block are left out of precision and counted on their own (Section 6.2).
  • F1 score: one number that is high only when both recall and precision are high.

We call events joined by {{bench.participation_pct.0}}%, {{bench.participation_pct.1}}% and {{bench.participation_pct.2}}% of cells large, mid-sized and small, and report recall for each. A small event has 3 cells; CoactDetect, LoCo and binned SCE need at least 3 cells to call, so small events sit right at their limit.

The {{tol_s}}-second match distance was chosen where LoCo's and CoactDetect's F1 scores stop rising as the distance widens. It is generous: a planted event's cells usually start within about 1 second. A generous distance rewards wide calls. Differences in the second decimal place of a score mean little.

6.2 A limit on false alarms where nothing was planted

Because busy-block calls are left out of precision, F1 cannot see them. So each hand-written detector has a limit on false alarms per minute in the busy block: {{ceilings.coact|g}} for CoactDetect, LoCo and SPIKE-synch, {{ceilings.rate|g}} for rate+context, {{ceilings.sce|g}} for binned SCE and {{ceilings.cicada|g}} for locust. Each limit was set above that detector's own earlier measured rate, so it flags a detector that has drifted worse than its own past; it does not compare detectors. The learned detectors have no limit yet. How the busy block should count toward a detector's grade is still an open question for the project. The limit says nothing about the opposite mistake, a detector that makes no calls in the busy block because it cannot find anything there; Figure 13 measures that separately.

6.3 Choosing a setting without cheating

Each hand-written detector has one main setting that raises or lowers its bar, and we try every value on a list; its other settings (bin widths, windows, merge gaps) were inherited and never tuned. A fair test must not choose the setting on the recordings it is scored on. We made {{rounds.n_recordings}} bench recordings and split them into {{rounds.n_folds}} groups of {{rounds.per_fold}} (Figure 11B). In each of {{rounds.n_folds}} rounds, the setting with the best F1 on the other {{rounds.n_choose}} recordings is scored on the {{rounds.per_fold}} it never saw. When two values tie, the looser one (a lower bar, so more calls) wins. In each round, each learned detector is trained three times, each from a different random start, using 12 of those {{rounds.n_choose}} recordings: 10 to learn from and 2 to set its call level. In one of the three runs the random start also chose different recordings.

The rounds choose by F1 alone. The project also has a stricter rule that refuses a setting at the end of its list, a setting from a list where every value scores the same, and a setting that breaks the busy-block limit. We did not use it here, so Section 7 reports some settings it would refuse.

We also score every hand-written detector at the setting it ships with, which Figures 3 to 8 and the real recordings use. Those settings were not chosen on these 24 recordings, but some depend on the same recipe: LoCo's is the best F1 on this bench, and locust's percentile was re-chosen on these two backgrounds. CoactDetect's and SPIKE-synch's are defaults from the lab's earlier viewing program. All of them are provisional.

10. What this means, and what could be wrong

What this means for the lab's question. {{PROSE:s10_meaning_real}} On simulated recordings, no detector is shown to be best. And every detector's calls move with how busy the cells are: some stop finding real events in a busy stretch, some call all through it, and some become more sensitive. So a detector's calls cannot, on their own, show that a drug changed coordination; a drug that makes cells busier changes the calls too. A study of a drug would need a way to separate the two, such as checking each detector against simulated busy stretches matched to the drug period's own activity. This document proposes no such method.

What could be wrong with the conclusions above.

  • Every accuracy number: it comes from simulated fast-stream recordings with {{bench.n_roi}} cells, whose busiest cells bunch far more than real ones, whose lineups are rarer, and whose timing scatter is poorly measured (Section 5).
  • "Several score about the same": no statistical test, a generous match distance chosen where the leaders stop improving, and decoys that cap F1 (Sections 6, 7).
  • binned SCE's standing: the official scores understate it through a scoring mismatch (Table 3, note ²).
  • The busy-stretch results: Figure 13 uses one kind of busy stretch (extra random events in every cell) at two intensities. Real drug periods may be busy in other ways, such as a change of state shared by all cells over tens of seconds, which these detectors do not count as coordination.
  • The learned detectors' blindness: it may reflect training that never showed an event inside a busy stretch, and it was measured on single versions (Sections 4, 7).
  • Anything about crowded events: planted events are always 2 minutes apart, which hides a known weakness of the detectors that judge chance locally (Sections 3.3, 5). The project has a crowded test recording for this; it was not run here.
  • SPIKE-synch's weaknesses: its limit and bar are this project's choices (Section 3.6).
  • Anything about the settings: the shipped settings are provisional, some were chosen on this recipe, and the slow stream runs on fast-stream settings (Sections 6.3, 8).
  • The real-data figures: one recording per group per drug, chosen by size; Figure 1A was chosen as the recording where the detectors' counts differ most; no person checked the calls; agreement between detectors was not measured (Section 8).
  • All of Section 7: the official scores are not yet released.

What this document leaves out.

  • Whether a drug changes coordination. Nothing here compares a drug period with baseline.
  • Methods that learn without an answer key. The lab is building detectors that learn from real recordings with no planted events; they are not ready.
  • A person's review of the calls, which is how the lab uses these detectors.

12. Word list

Calcium event
A brief brightening of one cell in the movie, which means the cell was active.
Coordinated event, lineup, stripe
A coordinated event is a moment when many cells are active together. A lineup is several cells with events at about the same time, whether by coordination or by chance. On a raster a lineup looks like a vertical stripe.
ROI (region of interest)
The patch of the movie treated as one cell. "ROI" and "cell" mean the same thing here.
Fast stream, slow stream
The lab's event-finding program keeps brief brightenings and much longer ones in two separate lists. We analyze each on its own.
Raster, lane
A raster is a picture of a recording with one row per cell and one tick per event. A lane is a strip above a raster that marks one detector's calls, or the planted events.
Detector, hand-written, learned, call
A detector is a program that marks coordinated events; each moment it marks is a call, a claim rather than a fact. A hand-written detector follows fixed rules a person wrote; a learned one adjusted its own numbers on examples.
Spike
The brief electrical signal a nerve cell sends.
Rate, mHz
How often a cell has events, in millihertz: 1 mHz is one event every 1,000 seconds.
Bin, frame, period
A bin is a short, fixed slice of time, such as 2 seconds. A frame is one picture of the movie, 0.1 seconds here. A period is one part of a recording: baseline, drug or high K+.
Surrogate, circular shift, shuffle
A surrogate is a scrambled copy of a recording used to see what chance gives. A circular shift slides each cell's whole row; a shuffle moves each event to a random time.
Bar, chance line
A bar is the value a detector's measure must pass before it makes a call. In Figure 13, the chance line is how often a call falls near a moment with no event at all.
Percentile
The 99th percentile of a list is the value that 99% of the list falls below.
Typical spread, variance
How far values usually stray from their average (the standard deviation); the variance is that spread squared.
Log scale
An axis on which each equal step multiplies the value by the same amount, so very different sizes fit on one plot.
Simulator, bench, seed, fitted
The simulator writes made-up recordings with a known answer; the bench is its standard recipe; a seed is the random starting number that makes one recording. "Fitted" means its settings were adjusted to match real baselines.
Planted event, decoy
A planted event is a coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose. A decoy is built the same way but left off the answer key.
Large, mid-sized and small events
Planted events joined by 30%, 18% and 10% of the cells.
Quiet background, busy background
The two background rates the bench runs at: {{bench.quiet_mhz}} and {{bench.busy_mhz|.0f}} mHz per cell, as set.
Busy block, busy stretch
The busy block is five minutes inside every bench recording, on both backgrounds, where every cell gets extra random events; normally nothing is planted there (Figure 13 is the exception). A busy stretch is any part of a recording, real or simulated, where cells are much busier than usual.
Busy-block limit
The most false alarms per minute in the busy block a hand-written detector is allowed (Section 6.2).
Hit, miss, false alarm
A hit is a call on a planted event; a miss is a planted event with no call; a false alarm is any other call.
Recall, precision, F1 score
Recall is the share of planted events found; precision is the share of calls that were hits; F1 is one score that is high only when both are.
Setting, shipped, tuned, loose, round
A setting is a number that controls a detector, such as how high its bar sits. The shipped setting is the one it comes with; a tuned setting is the one a round chose. A looser setting has a lower bar, so it makes more calls. A round is one pass of choosing a setting on some recordings and scoring it on others.
Official comparison run (bake-off)
The project's standard run that scores every detector through the rounds of Section 6.3.
Training, learning rate, call level
Training is adjusting a learned detector's numbers on examples, by steps of a chosen size (the learning rate). The call level is the output a moment must reach to become a call.
Filter, center, surround
A filter is a weighted average over nearby moments. The center filter looks at the moment scored; the surround filter looks at the seconds around it.
Baseline, TTX, senktide, high K+, marked windows
Baseline is the untreated start of a recording. TTX blocks fast electrical spikes; senktide switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor; high K+ (high potassium) makes healthy cells active. Marked windows are the stretches of each period the lab analyzes.
DI, MALE, ORX, OVX
The four groups of mice: females in diestrus, males, males with testes removed, and females with ovaries removed.
SCE, CICADA
SCE stands for synchronous calcium event. CICADA is calcium-imaging analysis software from the Cossart lab.
