Finding neurons that act together

How six computer programs decide when many neurons have calcium events at the same time, how we tested them, and why they sometimes disagree with your own eyes.

The short version

  • The lab records calcium signals in neurons using a microscope and a digital camera, and writes down the times of the calcium events in those signals. We want to ask whether calcium events happen in many neurons at around the same time. Computer programs have to find those moments, because there are far too many recordings to go through by hand.
  • Neurons have calcium events on their own as well, so some of them line up just by chance. Every program has to separate real teamwork from chance lineups, and each program uses a different set of steps to do that.
  • A real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which moments are real. So we also simulate recordings — we produce them ourselves, choosing in advance when the neurons act together — and check what each program finds against what we put in.
  • On simulated recordings, several programs score about the same. None of them is clearly best.

{{SEC:records}}. What the lab records

Figure {{NUM:fig_orient}} is the picture everything else in this document is built on: the stripes are what we are trying to find. Here is where it comes from.

The lab studies a small group of neurons called KNDy neurons. These neurons sit in the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the body's reproductive system (Lehman and others, 2010). The lab keeps thin slices of mouse brain alive in a dish and records them through a microscope with a digital camera, about ten pictures a second.

What makes a neuron visible? These mice carry an extra gene, so the neurons build a fluorescent protein of their own called GCaMP6f. Nothing is added to them; they make it themselves. The protein gives off more light when calcium is around it, so the camera sees each neuron's brightness follow the calcium inside it.

A calcium event is an increase followed by a decrease in one neuron's brightness. It is the whole of what we observe, and from here on this document calls it a calcium event, or just an event. It says the calcium inside that neuron went up. It does not say why, and this document does not assume why — which matters later, because one of the drugs in Section {{SEC:real}} blocks the electrical signals that are usually behind a calcium rise, and the events carry on regardless.

A separate program goes through the pictures, finds each calcium event and writes down the moment it happened. That program keeps two lists: one for brief events and one for much longer events.

Those lists of moments are all the programs in this document ever see. None of them looks at the pictures. We draw a list as a raster, the picture above: one row per neuron, one tick per event. A short, vertical stripe in a raster means many neurons had calcium events at nearly the same moment. That is a coordinated event, and finding coordinated events is the whole job.

{{SEC:problem}}. The problem

The six programs in this document, by name: rate+context, CoactDetect, LoCo, binned SCE, locust and SPIKE-synch. Who made each one is in the table in Section {{SEC:algorithms}}. Four of the six were built in this lab. locust is this project's modified version of the coordinated-event detector inside CICADA, a published analysis package from the Cossart lab (Denis and others, 2020); ours is fed the lab's own list of event times and uses its own bar, so its results are ours and not CICADA's. SPIKE-synch is built on a published mathematical measure of synchrony (Kreuz and others, 2015).

Figure {{NUM:fig_problem}} is a real recording. All six programs were given the same list of event times — the same raster, drawn at the bottom — and each program marked the moments where that program judged that neurons had acted together. One such mark is a call. A call is a claim by a program, not a fact about the neurons: it can be right or wrong, and nothing in the recording says which.

{{PROSE:problem_text}} A real recording has no answer key, so we cannot look up who is right. This document explains how each program decides, how we tested the programs on recordings whose answer we do know, and where the programs and a person's eye disagree.

{{SEC:chance}}. Lineups happen by chance

Why is finding coordinated events hard? Because neurons have calcium events on their own all the time, and when they have them often, some of them land close together by luck alone.

To count that, cut the recording into short stretches of equal length — a bin is one of those stretches, 2 seconds long in Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}} — and count how many different neurons had an event inside each one. Most programs in this document work from a count like that, and they differ in how long they make their bins. In Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}}B nothing at all was put in, and yet {{T.busy_max}} neurons still had events inside the same 2-second bin.

So counting neurons is not enough. A program has to ask a harder question: are more neurons lining up right now than chance alone would produce? To answer it, the program needs to know what chance looks like in this particular recording.

{{SEC:copies}}. Measuring chance with shifted copies

Four of the six programs measure chance the same basic way, the way Figure {{NUM:fig_chance_steps}} shows. The program makes shifted copies of the recording. To make one copy, it slides each neuron's whole row of events left or right by that neuron's own random amount; events pushed off one end come back in at the other end.

A shifted copy keeps everything about each neuron on its own: how many events that neuron has, and whether its events come in bursts or at a steady beat. What a shift destroys is the timing between neurons. So a lineup that survives in a copy is, as far as the shift can tell, a lineup that chance can produce. Counting lineups in a hundred copies tells the program how high a real count has to be before it means anything. Section {{SEC:shuffle}} is about why this particular way of making copies, and about where it falls short.

This is called a circular shift in the literature. "Shift" because each row slides; "circular" because the recording is treated as a loop, so what falls off one end comes back at the other. If you go looking for the papers behind these programs — they are listed at the end, under Where the ideas come from — that is the phrase to search for.

{{SEC:shuffle}}. Why shift, not shuffle

There is another common way to make a chance copy: shuffle the recording. A shuffle keeps the number of events each neuron has, but drops every one of them at a brand-new random time. That sounds fair, and it is not: a shuffle also destroys each neuron's own pattern, and then the chance bar comes out wrong. Figure {{NUM:fig_shift_shuffle}} shows the bar going wrong in both directions.

  • Neurons that fire in bursts (A). A shuffle breaks each burst apart and spreads the events out. Now more neurons land in any given bin by chance, so the bar comes out too high. A real burst of {{T.bursty_cells}} neurons, which shifted copies reach only {{T.bursty_shift}} of the time, is reached by {{T.bursty_shuffle}} of shuffled copies. The shuffle calls it chance and misses a real event.
  • Neurons that fire at a steady beat (B). A shuffle lets a neuron's events pile up in some bins and leave others empty. Now fewer neurons reach any bin by chance, so the bar comes out too low. Here {{T.even_cells}} neurons land in one bin purely by luck. Shifted copies do that {{T.even_shift}} of the time, so the shift correctly says "chance". Shuffled copies do it only {{T.even_shuffle}} of the time, so the shuffle calls a chance lineup.

The lab's own brief events behave like the steady-beat neurons (C). In {{T.n_baselines}} untreated recordings, a shuffle drops {{T.doubles_shuffle}} of every 1,000 events into a bin their own neuron had already filled, against {{T.doubles_real}} in the real recording and {{T.doubles_shift}} after a shift. As a result, shuffled copies say 6 or more neurons line up in {{T.n6_shuffle}} of bins, and shifted copies say {{T.n6_shift}}. A bar built from shuffles would sit too low. That is why every program here that makes copies uses the shift instead.

The shift is the better choice, not the right answer

It would be convenient if a shifted copy were a real recording with the coordination taken out and nothing else changed. It is not, and it is worth saying so plainly.

  • Real recordings line up more than the shift expects, even where nothing is coordinated. Eight or more neurons line up about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often in the recordings as in their shifted copies. Some of that is probably slow rises and falls that many neurons share, which a shift breaks along with everything else.
  • A shifted copy carries traces of having been shifted. In this project's own tests, a program trained to tell a real recording from a shifted copy of it can sometimes do so. The shift leaves a signature of its own, so a copy is not a silent, invisible version of the recording.

So treat the shift as what it is: an idea that clearly helps, and that beats the obvious alternative, rather than a solved problem. Every bar in this document rests on it, and that is a limit on every number that follows.

{{SEC:algorithms}}. The six algorithms

An algorithm is a fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that the computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs here are algorithms of this kind, and all six ask the same question, are more neurons lining up right now than chance explains?, but the steps differ. The difference that matters most is where each program looks to learn what chance is:

That last column decides how a program behaves when the neurons get busy, so it is worth following through slowly.

CoactDetect, LoCo and rate+context work from the surrounding seconds. Each of them measures chance from the minute around the moment it is testing — by making shifted copies of that minute, or, for rate+context, by averaging it. When the neurons in that minute have events more often, those copies produce bigger counts, and the count a bin has to beat is computed from them, so it goes up as well. A chance lineup inside a busy stretch therefore fails to beat it. The cost is that a small real coordinated event in the same stretch has to beat that raised count too, and often cannot.

binned SCE and locust work from the whole recording at once. Each of them measures chance one time, from copies of the entire stretch it was given, and uses the single count that comes out of it everywhere. That count was fixed before the busy stretch arrived and does not move when it does, so every bin in the busy stretch can beat it, one after another.

{{SEC:sim}}. Simulated recordings, where we know the answer

Section {{SEC:problem}} left us with the problem that a real recording has no answer key. Here is the way around it. We simulate recordings: a program of our own writes out lists of event times in the same form the lab's event-finder produces, and we choose in advance when the neurons act together. Since we put those moments in, we know where every one of them is, and we can check what each program finds against what we put in.

The program that writes them is the simulator. Each simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons and lasts {{T.minutes}} minutes (Figure {{NUM:fig_simulator}}), and it is built in layers:

  • Background: each neuron fires on its own, at its own rate. A few neurons are busy and many are nearly silent, as in real recordings. We made two versions: a quiet background (about {{T.quiet_per_hour}} events per neuron per hour) and a busy background (about {{T.busy_per_hour}}). Those match the quieter and busier untreated recordings in the lab's data ({{T.real_p25_per_hour}} and {{T.real_p75_per_hour}} per hour).
  • Planted events: {{T.n_planted}} coordinated events that we put in on purpose. They come in three sizes, counted by how many of the {{T.bench_n_roi}} neurons join in: {{T.big_cells}} neurons, {{T.mid_cells}} neurons, or {{T.small_cells}} neurons. {{T.mid_cells}} neurons is the size that matters — that is what the lab measured in real recordings, so it is the size a real coordinated event usually has. The {{T.small_cells}}-neuron size is the hardest case any program is asked to handle; three of the six will not call a lineup smaller than that at all. The neurons in one event start a little apart, not at exactly the same moment. Planted events are at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart.
  • A busy stretch: from minute {{T.hot_from}} to minute {{T.hot_to}}, every neuron gets about {{T.hot_per_hour}} extra random events per hour. Nothing is planted there, so any call there is a false alarm.
  • Decoys: {{T.n_decoys}} lineups built exactly like planted events, and deliberately left off the answer key.

Why we plant lineups and then refuse to count them

Decoys are the part of the recipe that surprises people, so here is the reasoning.

The easiest way to score well on a test is to call everything. A program that marked every lineup it saw would find all {{T.n_planted}} planted events and look excellent, while telling us nothing about whether it can separate coordination from coincidence. Decoys take that route away: they look exactly like the real thing, so calling everything now costs as many wrong answers as it buys right ones.

They also stand for something real. In a live recording some lineups genuinely are coincidence — neurons that happen to go off together, or that share a slow drift in how often they have events, with nothing coordinating them. A person reading the raster would not count those. Decoys put that same population of "real lineups that should not count" into the test.

The price is worth stating plainly: a program that sees only event times cannot tell a decoy from a planted event. They are built the same way. So the test has a ceiling — the best possible overall score is about {{T.ceiling}} instead of 1.0, and no program in this document could reach the top no matter how good it was. That ceiling is deliberate. It is the part of the problem that no cleverness inside a program can solve, and keeping it in the test stops us from reading a high score as a solved problem.

What the simulator gets wrong, and why it is still wrong

Four things. The obvious question is why we do not simply fix them, and the four answers are different, which is the point worth taking away.

  1. Its busiest neurons bunch their events together far more than real busy neurons do. Count each neuron's events in 30-second stretches and see how much those counts vary: 1 means as even as chance allows. Real busy neurons sit near {{T.bunch_real_busy}}; the simulator's sit near {{T.bunch_sim_busy}}. Why it stands: we know the cause — the simulator makes each neuron's rate rise and fall by the same relative swing, so the busier the neuron, the wilder the swing — and a better background model is open work. It is not a patch, because every score in this document was measured against this background, so changing it means running the whole test again.
  2. Its large lineups are rarer than real ones. In real recordings, 8 or more neurons line up about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often as chance predicts; the simulator does not reproduce that. Why it stands: this is the hard one. The simulator can only contain coordination we already know how to describe — so many neurons, starting within so many tenths of a second. Whatever produces the big lineups in real recordings has not been described yet. Building it into the simulator would mean already knowing the answer these programs are supposed to find.
  3. Every simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons, while real ones run from about half that to more, and several of the programs have rules that depend on how many neurons there are. Why it stands: this one is genuinely easy — the count is a setting. It has not been changed because changing it would make these scores incomparable with the ones before them. The right fix is to run the whole test at several neuron counts and report the spread, which costs compute rather than thought.
  4. How far apart the neurons in a real coordinated event start is not well measured. Why it stands: the measurement does not yet work. Run it on a simulated recording built with a known spread and it does not return that spread, so there is nothing trustworthy to copy from the real recordings. A better estimator has to come first.

Underneath three of those four is the same limit: a simulator can only hold what we already know how to write down. The parts of a real recording we most want to test a program against are exactly the parts nobody can describe yet. That is not a reason to stop using simulated recordings — they are the only place an answer key exists — but it is the reason a good score here is not a promise about real data, and the reason Section {{SEC:real}} exists.

{{SEC:grading}}. How we grade a program

Grading compares each program's calls with the answer key. A call that lands within {{T.tol}} seconds of a planted event is a hit. A planted event with no call near it is a miss. Every other call is a false alarm, including a call on a decoy.

From those we get two numbers: the share of planted events a program found, and the share of its calls that were right. The overall score combines them into one number from 0 to 1. It is high only when both are high. (Its technical name is the F1 score.)

Calls inside the busy stretch are false alarms too, but they are kept out of the "right" share and counted on their own (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}, B). That stretch is busier than {{T.cnt_block_slower}} of the lab's {{T.cnt_n_real}} untreated recordings, and folded into the score it would mostly measure how hard the test was set. The cost is that the overall score cannot see a program that calls all the way through a busy stretch, so that has to be checked separately (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, B).

Most programs have one main setting that raises or lowers their bar. To choose it fairly, we made {{T.n_recordings_scored}} test recordings and split them into {{T.n_tests}} groups of {{T.per_test}}. For each group, we picked the best setting using the other {{T.n_choose}} recordings, then scored the program on the {{T.per_test}} it had never seen.

Those rounds happen only in Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}. Apart from that one figure, each program runs at a single stored value for its main setting — the same value on every recording, real or simulated, and the one that produced every call this project has ever reported. Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}} sets that stored value aside and lets each round pick its own; nothing else does.

So why not keep the settings the rounds picked? Because the rounds do not produce a setting. They produce {{T.n_tests}} of them, one per round, and for {{T.n_setting_disagree}} of the six programs those {{T.n_tests}} answers disagree with each other. locust is the clearest case: its rounds picked {{T.cicada_pick_lo}} and {{T.cicada_pick_hi}}, a thousandfold apart, from the same {{T.n_recordings_scored}} recordings. There is no single value there to install.

That disagreement is not a flaw in the rounds; it is what they were built to reveal. A round answers a narrow question — how well does this program do when its setting was picked without seeing the recordings it is graded on? — and the answer is a score, not a value. Two further reasons not to install one: any setting picked this way is picked against the simulator, whose faults Section {{SEC:sim}} lists, and real recordings have no answer key, so nothing can be tuned on them at all.

Those stored values did not all arrive the same way. For CoactDetect, LoCo and locust, somebody ran the program on simulated recordings and kept the value that scored best. CoactDetect's ended up a hundred times stricter than the value the program was first written with. For rate+context, binned SCE and SPIKE-synch, the stored value is the value the program was first written with — nobody has tuned it. So half of the six carry a tuned value and half do not, and the tuned three were tuned on recordings from the same simulator that made every test recording here.

{{SEC:scores}}. Scores on the simulated recordings

On the quiet background, three programs scored about the same (Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}): CoactDetect {{T.f1q_coact}}, LoCo {{T.f1q_loco}} and rate+context {{T.f1q_rate}}. The best possible was about {{T.ceiling}}. With this many test recordings we cannot say that any one of them is truly better than the others. binned SCE looks worse ({{T.f1q_sce}}), but most of that comes from how its calls are timed: when we score it over its whole 10-second bins, it reaches {{T.sce_fair_quiet}}. locust ({{T.f1q_cicada}}) and SPIKE-synch ({{T.f1q_sync}}) scored lower.

Busy stretches are where the programs split apart (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}). The recordings above never put a coordinated event inside the busy stretch, so a program that goes quiet there looks good even if it has gone blind. To find out which, we made {{T.block_recordings}} new recordings with coordinated events planted inside the busy stretch.

  • CoactDetect and LoCo, which judge chance from nearby time, stay quiet in the busy stretch, but they also miss most real events there: CoactDetect found {{T.out_coact}} of the {{T.mid_cells}}-neuron events outside it and {{T.in_coact}} inside.
  • locust and binned SCE, which judge chance from the whole recording, keep calling in the busy stretch: about {{T.fa_cicada}} and {{T.fa_sce}} false alarms per minute. binned SCE calls all the way through it, so its calls there tell us nothing.
  • rate+context did both jobs fairly well at this level of busyness ({{T.in_rate}} found inside, {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms per minute). When we made the stretch three times busier, none of the programs held up.

{{SEC:count}}. Why not just ask for a minimum number of events in an interval?

Every program in this document does a lot of work to decide what chance looks like. There is a much simpler rule: call a coordinated event whenever at least a set number of neurons have calcium events within a short interval of each other (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_rule}}). Each neuron counts once, however many events it has inside the interval. No copies, and no chance. So why not use it?

Labs that study these same neurons use rules like it. Allan Herbison's lab, recording brain slices as this lab does, calls a "synchronized event" whenever the event peaks of at least 2 neurons fall within 10 seconds of each other, and adds any further neuron whose peak falls within 10 seconds of the one before (Han and others, 2023). The same rule, still at 2 neurons, is used in the lab's slice papers through 2026 (Morris and Herbison, 2023; Eddleston and others, 2026). Aleisha Moore and coworkers, recording these neurons in living mice, called an episode a "synchronized episode" when every recorded neuron had a calcium event (Moore, Coolen and Lehman, 2022). None of these papers checks its rule against chance. This section does, with an interval of {{T.cnt_w}} seconds — the length CoactDetect counts in — and with Herbison's rule as published.

On simulated recordings, the rule can score well

With the right bar, the rule scores about as well as the best programs. With a bar of {{T.cnt_best_quiet}} neurons it scored {{T.cnt_f1_best_quiet}} on the quiet background; CoactDetect scored {{T.cnt_coact_f1_quiet}} on the same recordings (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, A). That is a real result. It also flatters the rule a little, because its bar was picked on these very recordings, with the answers in hand.

But the right bar depends on the recording. On the busy background the best bar is {{T.cnt_best_busy}} neurons. Use the quiet background's bar of {{T.cnt_best_quiet}} there and the score falls to {{T.cnt_quiet_bar_on_busy}}. The one bar that does best on both backgrounds together, {{T.cnt_one_bar}} neurons, scores {{T.cnt_f1_one_quiet}} on the quiet background and {{T.cnt_f1_one_busy}} on the busy one.

And the bar that scores best calls most of the busy stretch, where nothing was planted (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, B). At its best bar, the rule called {{T.cnt_empty_best_quiet}} of that stretch on the quiet background and {{T.cnt_empty_best_busy}} on the busy one. On the busy background, CoactDetect called {{T.cnt_coact_empty_busy}} of the stretch and LoCo {{T.cnt_loco_empty_busy}}. A 10-second interval did no better: its best bars ({{T.cnt10_best_quiet}} neurons on the quiet background, {{T.cnt10_best_busy}} on the busy one) scored {{T.cnt10_f1_quiet}} and {{T.cnt10_f1_busy}}, and called at least {{T.cnt10_empty}} of the stretch. Herbison's rule, run exactly as published, called at least {{T.herb_empty_busy}} of it and scored {{T.herb_f1_quiet}} on the quiet background and {{T.herb_f1_busy}} on the busy one — though our simulated recordings hold {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons, more than the 8 to 29 in view in that lab's slices, and a rule built for fewer neurons is reached more easily with more.

The overall score did not show this, because calls inside the busy stretch are kept out of it and counted on their own (Section {{SEC:grading}}). Two of the six programs have the same weakness: binned SCE, which counts neurons in bins and sets one bar from the whole recording, called {{T.cnt_sce_empty_busy}} of the stretch, and locust called {{T.cnt_cicada_empty_busy}}. CoactDetect and LoCo avoid it by setting their bar from the minutes around each moment, so a busy stretch raises its own bar.

Why an interval and not a bin

An even simpler version of the rule cuts the recording into fixed bins and counts the neurons in each. It has a flaw of its own: a coordinated event that falls across the edge between two bins is split, some of its neurons landing in one bin and the rest in the next, so neither bin may reach the bar. With a bar of {{T.edge_x}} neurons, fixed {{T.edge_bin}}-second bins found {{T.edge_bin_at}} of the 6-neuron events that fell close to an edge (within an eighth of a bin), against {{T.edge_bin_mid}} of those in the middle of a bin. A window that slides along with the events cannot split one, and found {{T.edge_int_at}} of them wherever they fell (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_edge}}). Herbison's rule slides in this way, which is one thing it gets right. Two of the six programs count in fixed bins and pay a smaller price for it: CoactDetect found {{T.edge_coact_at}} of the events at an edge against {{T.edge_coact_mid}} mid-bin, and LoCo {{T.edge_loco_at}} against {{T.edge_loco_mid}}.

On real recordings, no single bar fits

Real recordings differ far more than the two simulated backgrounds do. Across the lab's {{T.cnt_n_real}} untreated recordings, neurons had between {{T.cnt_rate_min}} and {{T.cnt_rate_max}} events per neuron per minute on average — between {{T.cnt_rate_p10}} and {{T.cnt_rate_p90}} in eight recordings out of ten — and a recording held {{T.cnt_cells_min}} to {{T.cnt_cells_max}} neurons. The bar that holds chance to less than once in 10 minutes climbs with how busy the neurons are, from {{T.cnt_need_min}} neurons to {{T.cnt_need_max}} (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_slices}}). A bar of {{T.cnt_one_bar}} neurons lets chance through in {{T.cnt_n_loose}} of the {{T.cnt_n_real}} recordings — in one of them {{T.cnt_loose_max}} times every 10 minutes — while in {{T.cnt_n_strict}} recordings a bar of 3 neurons would already have been enough.

That is the real trouble with a fixed bar. A drug that gives neurons more calcium events moves a recording to the right in Figure {{NUM:fig_count_slices}}. With a fixed bar, that recording then shows more "coordinated events" even if nothing about how the neurons work together has changed.

At the published rate, what does chance give?

None of these papers compares its rule with chance, so we made the simplest version of that comparison (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_published}}). In the most recent paper, neurons had {{T.pub_rep_rate}} calcium events per neuron per hour, and the paper reports {{T.pub_rep_mse}} "synchronized events" per neuron per hour, each joined by {{T.pub_rep_cells}} neurons ({{T.pub_rep_share}}% of the neurons in view) on average (Eddleston and others, 2026). Neurons that fire completely independently at that rate, in a field of {{T.pub_field}} neurons — about the average those two numbers imply — meet the rule {{T.pub_chance_mse}} times per neuron per hour, with {{T.pub_chance_cells}} neurons in each.

So at that rate, independent neurons alone produce about as many "synchronized events" as the paper reports, but smaller ones: the events the paper found hold more neurons than chance events do. Real neurons do not fire at a steady rate, and none of the paper's data went into this, so it is not a verdict on its findings. It shows why a count needs a chance comparison beside it: without one, there is no way to tell how much of the count chance alone would have produced.

What this means

Asking for a minimum number of neurons within an interval is not wrong, and on quiet simulated recordings it does about as well as any program here. What a fixed bar cannot do is tell a busy stretch from a coordinated one, because the bar does not move when the neurons get busier. Counting in fixed bins instead of a sliding interval adds a second problem, the bin edge, and two of the six programs share it. Shifted copies (Section {{SEC:copies}}) let a program set its bar from the recording's own chance, and CoactDetect and LoCo go one step further, setting it from the minutes around each moment. That is what the extra work buys.

A real recording has no answer key. The figures in this section show where each program made calls. No figure here can show whether a call is right.

Four extra rows, and they are not part of this review. The figures in this section and the next carry {{T.n_learned}} rows whose names begin with tube. Those are learned models — programs that were shown examples and worked out their own rule, rather than following steps somebody wrote. They are still being built, none of this document's {{T.n_coded}} algorithms is one of them, and nothing here tests or judges them. They are drawn only so the rows are not silently missing. Treat them as work in progress; a separate document covers them.

The lab tests two drugs. TTX (tetrodotoxin) blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve neurons use. Senktide switches on a sensor these neurons carry, the neurokinin 3 receptor (Navarro and others, 2009). Many recordings end with high potassium, which makes healthy neurons produce calcium events, so a neuron that stays dark through it was probably never going to show us anything. One might expect TTX to stop all teamwork between neurons, which would give us a ready-made answer key. In this lab's recordings TTX does not stop it: events and lineups continue under the drug. So a TTX recording cannot serve as a "no coordination" test.

The lab marks analysis windows: the stretches of each recording it studies. Four of the six programs run only inside those windows. LoCo and locust run on the whole recording.

{{SEC:eye}}. Where your eye and the programs disagree

Two things stand out in Figures {{NUM:real_ttx_brief}} to {{NUM:real_senk_long}}. Sharp stripes that anyone can see are sometimes missed by most programs, and some calls sit where the raster shows nothing special. Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}} looks closely at both.

The second half of that rule is what makes the ▼ marks match what a person would point at. Inside a busy stretch, a quarter of the neurons often line up by chance alone (Section {{SEC:chance}}), and nobody reading the raster would call those stripes.

{{PROSE:eye_findings}}

{{SEC:means}}. What this means

  • No program is shown to be best. On simulated recordings, five score about the same.
  • Busy neurons change every program's answer. Some go blind in busy stretches, some call all through them. A drug that makes neurons busier can change the calls even if teamwork between neurons has not changed. Any study of a drug has to separate the two.
  • A call means "unusual for this recording", not "big". In a quiet recording, 3 neurons lining up can be rare enough to call. That is correct for the math and unconvincing to the eye.
    • {{PROSE:meaning_real}}

What could be wrong here

  • Every score comes from simulated recordings, and the simulator is imperfect (Section {{SEC:sim}}).
  • Every bar in this document comes from shifted copies, and a shifted copy is not a perfect stand-in for a recording with the coordination removed (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
  • We ran no statistical test between the top programs, and the decoys cap every score.
  • The "clear stripe" rule in Section {{SEC:eye}} is our own. A person may see stripes it misses, or miss some it counts.
  • The real recordings shown are one per group of mice, per drug. That is too few to say how common any of this is.
  • Planted events are always at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart. That hides a known weakness of the programs that judge chance from nearby time: two real events close together can raise each other's bar.

Word list

Algorithm
A fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that a computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs in this document are algorithms.
Analysis window
A stretch of a recording the lab marks for study. Some programs look only inside these windows.
Bar
The level a program's count must pass before it makes a call.
Bin
One of the short, equal stretches of time a recording is cut into before counting — 1, 2 or 10 seconds long, depending on the program.
Brief events, long events
The lab's event-finding program keeps short calcium events and much longer ones in two separate lists. Each list is studied on its own.
Calcium event
An increase followed by a decrease in one neuron's brightness: calcium inside that neuron went up and then cleared again. It is what we observe; what caused the rise is a separate question.
GCaMP6f
The fluorescent protein these neurons build themselves, from a gene the mice carry. It gives off more light when calcium is around it, which is what makes a rise in calcium visible under the microscope.
Call
One moment a program marks as a coordinated event. A call is that program's claim, not a fact about the neurons.
Clear stripe
Our rule for a lineup anyone can see on a raster (Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}}).
Coordinated event
A moment when many neurons have calcium events together, more than chance explains.
Decoy
A lineup put into a simulated recording the same way a planted event is, but left off the answer key.
False alarm
A call where there was no planted event.
High potassium
A solution that makes healthy neurons produce calcium events, often used at the end of a recording as a check that a quiet neuron could have shown something.
Overall score (F1 score)
One number from 0 to 1 that is high only when a program both finds most planted events and makes few false alarms.
Planted event
A coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose, at a time we chose.
Raster
A picture of a recording: one row per neuron, one tick per event.
Senktide
A drug that switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor, a sensor these neurons carry.
Shifted copy (a circular shift, in the research literature)
A copy of a recording in which each neuron's whole row of events is slid by that neuron's own random amount, with events pushed off one end coming back at the other. A shift keeps each neuron's own pattern and breaks only the timing between neurons.
Shuffled copy
A copy in which every event is moved to a new random time. A shuffle changes each neuron's own pattern as well, which is why it gets chance wrong (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
Simulate, simulator
To produce a recording ourselves instead of measuring one, choosing in advance when the neurons act together. The simulator is the program that writes those recordings.
"Synchronized event"
The term Allan Herbison's lab uses for a call of its interval rule: at least 2 neurons whose event peaks fall within 10 seconds of each other (Section {{SEC:count}}).
TTX (tetrodotoxin)
A drug that blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve neurons use.
