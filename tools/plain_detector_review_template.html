Finding cells that act together

How ten computer programs decide when many brain cells light up at the same time, how we tested them, and why they sometimes disagree with your own eyes.

The short version

  • The lab records brain cells through a microscope with a digital camera. A cell lights up when it is active. We want to find the moments when many cells light up together. Computer programs have to find those moments, because there are far too many recordings to go through by hand.
  • Cells also light up on their own, so some of them line up just by chance. Every program has to separate real teamwork from chance lineups, and each program uses a different set of steps to do that.
  • A real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which moments are real. So we also simulate recordings — we produce them ourselves, choosing in advance when the cells act together — and check what each program finds against what we put in.
  • On simulated recordings, several programs score about the same. None of them is clearly best.

1. What the lab records

Figure {{NUM:fig_raster}} is the picture everything else in this document is built on. Here is where it comes from.

The lab studies a small group of brain cells called KNDy neurons. These cells sit in the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the body's reproductive system (Lehman and others, 2010). The lab keeps thin slices of mouse brain alive in a dish and records them through a microscope with a digital camera, about ten pictures a second.

Each cell holds a dye that glows brighter when the cell is active. A short brightening is a calcium event. A separate program goes through the pictures, finds each brightening and writes down the moment it happened. That program keeps two lists: one for brief events and one for much longer events.

Those lists of moments are all the programs in this document ever see. None of them looks at the pictures. We draw a list as a raster, the picture above: one row per cell, one tick per event. A short stripe in a raster means many cells lit up at nearly the same moment. That is a coordinated event, and finding coordinated events is the whole job.

2. The problem

Figure {{NUM:fig_problem}} is a real recording. All six programs were given the same list of event times — the same raster, drawn at the bottom — and each program marked the moments where that program judged that cells had acted together. One such mark is a call. A call is a claim by a program, not a fact about the cells: it can be right or wrong, and nothing in the recording says which.

{{PROSE:problem_text}} A real recording has no answer key, so we cannot look up who is right. This document explains how each program decides, how we tested the programs on recordings whose answer we do know, and where the programs and a person's eye disagree.

3. Lineups happen by chance

Why is finding coordinated events hard? Because cells light up on their own all the time, and when they do it often, some of them land close together by luck alone. In Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}}B nothing was put in, and yet {{T.busy_max}} cells still light up inside the same 2-second piece.

So counting cells is not enough. A program has to ask a harder question: are more cells lining up right now than chance alone would produce? To answer it, the program needs to know what chance looks like in this particular recording.

4. Measuring chance with shifted copies

Four of the six programs measure chance the same basic way, the way Figure {{NUM:fig_chance_steps}} shows. The program makes shifted copies of the recording. To make one copy, it slides each cell's whole row of events left or right by that cell's own random amount; events pushed off one end come back in at the other end.

A shifted copy keeps everything about each cell on its own: how often that cell lights up, and whether its events come in bursts or at a steady beat. What a shift destroys is the timing between cells. So a lineup that survives in a copy is a lineup chance can produce. Counting lineups in a hundred copies tells the program how high a real count has to be before it means anything.

5. Why shift, not shuffle

There is another common way to make a chance copy: shuffle the recording. A shuffle keeps the number of events each cell has, but drops every one of them at a brand-new random time. That sounds fair, and it is not: a shuffle also destroys each cell's own pattern, and then the chance bar comes out wrong. Figure {{NUM:fig_shift_shuffle}} shows the bar going wrong in both directions.

  • Cells that fire in bursts (A). A shuffle breaks each burst apart and spreads the events out. Now more cells land in any given piece by chance, so the bar comes out too high. A real burst of {{T.bursty_cells}} cells, which shifted copies reach only {{T.bursty_shift}} of the time, is reached by {{T.bursty_shuffle}} of shuffled copies. The shuffle calls it chance and misses a real event.
  • Cells that fire at a steady beat (B). A shuffle lets a cell's events pile up in some pieces and leave others empty. Now fewer cells reach any piece by chance, so the bar comes out too low. Here {{T.even_cells}} cells land in one piece purely by luck. Shifted copies do that {{T.even_shift}} of the time, so the shift correctly says "chance". Shuffled copies do it only {{T.even_shuffle}} of the time, so the shuffle calls a chance lineup.

The lab's own brief events behave like the steady-beat cells (C). In {{T.n_baselines}} untreated recordings, a shuffle drops {{T.doubles_shuffle}} of every 1,000 events into a piece their own cell had already filled, against {{T.doubles_real}} in the real recording and {{T.doubles_shift}} after a shift. As a result, shuffled copies say 6 or more cells line up in {{T.n6_shuffle}} of pieces, and shifted copies say {{T.n6_shift}}. A bar built from shuffles would sit too low. That is why every program here that makes copies uses the shift.

Even the shift is not perfect. Real recordings have more very large lineups than shifted copies predict: 8 or more cells line up about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often as the copies say. Part of that may be slow ups and downs that many cells share, which shifting also breaks.

6. The six algorithms

An algorithm is a fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that the computer carries out in the same order every time. Six of the ten programs are algorithms of this kind. All six ask the same question, are more cells lining up right now than chance explains?, but the steps differ. The difference that matters most is where each program looks to learn what chance is:

algorithmwhat it countswhere it learns what chance looks like
rate+contextevents per second, from all cells added upthe average of the nearby minute (no copies)
CoactDetectcells lit in each 2-second pieceshifted copies of the nearby minute
LoCocells lit in each 1-second pieceshifted copies of the minute before and the minute after
binned SCEcells lit in each 10-second pieceshifted copies of the whole stretch being studied
locustcells "switched on" in each 0.1-second frameshifted copies of the whole recording
SPIKE-synchhow close each event is to events in other cellsno copies: each cell's own gaps between events

Looking nearby means the bar rises when cells get busy, so chance lineups in a busy stretch are not called. The cost is that a small real event in a busy stretch can be missed too. Looking at the whole recording gives one bar for everything, so a busy stretch can pass it again and again. Each figure has two views: a quiet minute holding one real coordinated event (A), and a minute inside a busy stretch where nothing was put in, so every call there is a mistake (B).

7. The neural networks

The four remaining programs were not written as steps at all. Each of the four is a small neural network: a stack of simple arithmetic operations, each holding numbers the computer can adjust. We showed the network many simulated recordings whose answer we knew. After each example, the computer nudged those numbers so the network's output rose at real coordinated events and fell everywhere else. That nudging is training. When training ends the numbers are fixed, and the network runs like any other program: event times in, calls out.

This network's design is called tube, from the picture its author had in mind: looking down a dark tube while the recording slides past, a coordinated event goes by as a bright flash. Figure {{NUM:fig_network}} follows one recording through each stage of the network, with the real signal at every stage.

There are four versions. tube is the one drawn above. tube-guard ignores the brightness right next to the moment it scores, so a real event does not push up its own comparison. tube-ratio divides by the surrounding brightness instead of subtracting it. tube-ratio-guard does both. All four were trained only on simulated recordings, and they were taught that busy stretches never hold a real coordinated event.

8. Simulated recordings, where we know the answer

Section 2 left us with the problem that a real recording has no answer key. Here is the way around it. We simulate recordings: a program of our own writes out lists of event times in the same form the lab's event-finder produces, and we choose in advance when the cells act together. Since we put those moments in, we know where every one of them is, and we can check what each program finds against what we put in.

The program that writes them is the simulator. Each simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells and lasts {{T.minutes}} minutes (Figure {{NUM:fig_simulator}}), and it is built in layers:

  • Background: each cell fires on its own, at its own rate. A few cells are busy and many are nearly silent, as in real recordings. We made two versions: a quiet background (about {{T.quiet_per_hour}} events per cell per hour) and a busy background (about {{T.busy_per_hour}}). Those match the quieter and busier untreated recordings in the lab's data ({{T.real_p25_per_hour}} and {{T.real_p75_per_hour}} per hour).
  • Planted events: {{T.n_planted}} coordinated events that we put in on purpose, joined by 30%, 18% or 10% of the cells. The cells in one event start a little apart, not at exactly the same moment. Planted events are at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart.
  • A busy stretch: from minute {{T.hot_from}} to minute {{T.hot_to}}, every cell gets about {{T.hot_per_hour}} extra random events per hour. Nothing is planted there, so any call there is a false alarm.
  • Decoys: {{T.n_decoys}} lineups built exactly like planted events but left off the answer key. They stand in for lineups a careful person would not count. No program that sees only event times can tell a decoy from a real event, so even a perfect program could score only about {{T.ceiling}}.

What the simulator gets wrong. Its busiest cells bunch their events together far more than real busy cells do. Its large lineups are rarer than real ones. Every simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells, while real recordings run from about half that to more. And how far apart the cells in a real coordinated event start is not well measured. So a good score on simulated recordings does not promise a good score on real ones.

9. How we grade a program

Grading compares each program's calls with the answer key. A call that lands within {{T.tol}} seconds of a planted event is a hit. A planted event with no call near it is a miss. Every other call is a false alarm, including a call on a decoy.

From those we get two numbers: the share of planted events a program found, and the share of its calls that were right. The overall score combines them into one number from 0 to 1. It is high only when both are high. (Its technical name is the F1 score.)

Most programs have one main setting that raises or lowers their bar. To choose it fairly, we made {{T.n_recordings_scored}} test recordings and split them into {{T.n_tests}} groups of {{T.per_test}}. For each group, we picked the best setting using the other {{T.n_choose}} recordings, then scored the program on the {{T.per_test}} it had never seen. The neural networks were trained the same way, never on the recordings they were scored on.

10. Scores on the simulated recordings

On the quiet background, five programs scored about the same (Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}): CoactDetect {{T.f1q_coact}}, LoCo {{T.f1q_loco}}, tube-guard {{T.f1q_tube_guard}}, tube {{T.f1q_tube}} and rate+context {{T.f1q_rate}}. The best possible was about {{T.ceiling}}. With this many test recordings we cannot say that any one of them is truly better than the others. binned SCE looks worse ({{T.f1q_sce}}), but most of that comes from how its calls are timed: when we score it over its whole 10-second pieces, it reaches {{T.sce_fair_quiet}}. locust ({{T.f1q_cicada}}) and SPIKE-synch ({{T.f1q_sync}}) scored lower.

Busy stretches are where the programs split apart (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}). The recordings above never put a coordinated event inside the busy stretch, so a program that goes quiet there looks good even if it has gone blind. To find out which, we made {{T.block_recordings}} new recordings with coordinated events planted inside the busy stretch.

  • CoactDetect and LoCo, which judge chance from nearby time, stay quiet in the busy stretch, but they also miss most real events there: CoactDetect found {{T.out_coact}} of mid-sized events outside it and {{T.in_coact}} inside.
  • locust and binned SCE, which judge chance from the whole recording, keep calling in the busy stretch: about {{T.fa_cicada}} and {{T.fa_sce}} false alarms per minute. binned SCE calls all the way through it, so its calls there tell us nothing.
  • rate+context did both jobs fairly well at this level of busyness ({{T.in_rate}} found inside, {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms per minute). When we made the stretch three times busier, none of the programs held up.

11. The programs on real recordings

A real recording has no answer key. The figures in this section show where each program made calls. No figure here can show whether a call is right.

The lab tests two drugs. TTX (tetrodotoxin) blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve cells use. Senktide switches on a sensor these cells carry, the neurokinin 3 receptor (Navarro and others, 2009). Many recordings end with high potassium, which makes healthy cells active. One might expect TTX to stop all teamwork between cells, which would give us a ready-made answer key. In this lab's recordings TTX does not stop it: events and lineups continue under the drug. So a TTX recording cannot serve as a "no coordination" test.

The lab marks analysis windows: the stretches of each recording it studies. Six of the ten programs run only inside those windows. LoCo and locust run on the whole recording.

12. Where your eye and the programs disagree

Two things stand out in Figures {{NUM:real_ttx_brief}} to {{NUM:real_senk_long}}. Sharp stripes that anyone can see are sometimes missed by most programs, and some calls sit where the raster shows nothing special. Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}} looks closely at both.

The second half of that rule is what makes the ▼ marks match what a person would point at. Inside a busy stretch, a quarter of the cells often line up by chance alone (Section 3), and nobody reading the raster would call those stripes.

13. What this means

  • No program is shown to be best. On simulated recordings, five score about the same.
  • Busy cells change every program's answer. Some go blind in busy stretches, some call all through them. A drug that makes cells busier can change the calls even if teamwork between cells has not changed. Any study of a drug has to separate the two.
  • A call means "unusual for this recording", not "big". In a quiet recording, 3 cells lining up can be rare enough to call. That is correct for the math and unconvincing to the eye.
    • {{PROSE:meaning_real}}

What could be wrong here

  • Every score comes from simulated recordings, and the simulator is imperfect (Section 8).
  • We ran no statistical test between the top programs, and the decoys cap every score.
  • The neural networks were taught that busy stretches never hold a real event, which may be why some of them go blind there.
  • The "clear stripe" rule in Section 12 is our own. A person may see stripes it misses, or miss some it counts.
  • The real recordings shown are one per group of mice, per drug. That is too few to say how common any of this is.
  • Planted events are always at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart. That hides a known weakness of the programs that judge chance from nearby time: two real events close together can raise each other's bar.

A fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that a computer carries out in the same order every time. Six of the ten programs here are algorithms.
Analysis window
A stretch of a recording the lab marks for study. Some programs look only inside these windows.
Bar
The level a program's count must pass before it makes a call.
Brief events, long events
The lab's event-finding program keeps short brightenings and much longer ones in two separate lists. Each list is studied on its own.
Calcium event
A short brightening of one cell in the recording, which means the cell was active.
Call
One moment a program marks as a coordinated event. A call is that program's claim, not a fact about the cells.
Clear stripe
Our rule for a lineup anyone can see on a raster (Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}}).
Coordinated event
A moment when many cells become active together, more than chance explains.
Decoy
A lineup put into a simulated recording the same way a planted event is, but left off the answer key.

A call where there was no planted event.
High potassium
A solution that makes healthy cells active, often used at the end of a recording.
Neural network
A stack of simple arithmetic steps whose numbers were adjusted by training on examples, instead of being written by a person.
Overall score (F1 score)
One number from 0 to 1 that is high only when a program both finds most planted events and makes few false alarms.
Planted event
A coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose, at a time we chose.
Raster
A picture of a recording: one row per cell, one tick per event.
Senktide
A drug that switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor, a sensor these cells carry.
Shifted copy
A copy of a recording in which each cell's whole row of events is slid by that cell's own random amount. A shift keeps each cell's own pattern and breaks only the timing between cells.
Shuffled copy
A copy in which every event is moved to a new random time. A shuffle changes each cell's own pattern as well, which is why it gets chance wrong (Section 5).
Simulate, simulator
To produce a recording ourselves instead of measuring one, choosing in advance when the cells act together. The simulator is the program that writes those recordings.
Training
Adjusting a neural network's numbers, example by example, until its output matches the answer key.
TTX (tetrodotoxin)
A drug that blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve cells use.
