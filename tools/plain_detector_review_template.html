Finding neurons that act together

How six computer programs decide when many neurons have calcium events at the same time, how we tested them, and why they sometimes disagree with your own eyes.

The short version

  • The lab records calcium signals in neurons using a microscope and a digital camera, and writes down the times of the calcium events in those signals. We want to ask whether calcium events happen in many neurons at around the same time, which this document calls a coordinated event. Computer programs have to find those moments, because there are far too many recordings to go through by hand.
  • Neurons have calcium events on their own as well, so some coordinated events happen just by chance. Every program has to separate the coordinated events chance explains from the ones it does not, and each program uses a different set of steps to do that.
  • A real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which moments are real. So we also simulate recordings — we produce them ourselves, choosing in advance when the neurons act together — and check what each program finds against what we put in.
  • On simulated recordings, CoactDetect and LoCo do best on that test, and we cannot tell those two apart. Neither is best at everything: when we plant coordinated events inside a stretch where every neuron is busy, both miss most of them.

{{SEC:records}}. What the lab records

Figure {{NUM:fig_orient}} is the picture everything else in this document is built on: the stripes are what we are trying to find.

The lab records a group of neurons called KNDy neurons in thin slices of mouse brain kept alive in a dish, through a microscope with a digital camera, about ten pictures a second.

What makes a neuron visible? These mice carry an extra gene, so the neurons build a fluorescent protein of their own called GCaMP6f. Nothing is added to them; they make it themselves. The protein gives off more light when calcium is around it, so the camera sees each neuron's brightness follow the calcium inside it.

A calcium event is an increase followed by a decrease in one neuron's brightness. It is the whole of what we observe, and from here on this document calls it a calcium event, or just an event. It says the calcium inside that neuron went up. It does not say why, and this document does not assume why — which matters later, because one of the drugs in Section {{SEC:real}} blocks the electrical signals that are usually behind a calcium rise, and the events carry on regardless.

A separate program goes through the pictures, finds each calcium event and writes down the moment it happened. That program keeps two lists: one for brief events and one for much longer events.

Those lists of moments are all the programs in this document ever see. None of them looks at the pictures. We draw a list as a raster, the picture above: one row per neuron, one tick per event. A narrow, vertical stripe in a raster means many neurons had calcium events at nearly the same moment. That is a coordinated event. Some coordinated events happen by chance alone, and telling those apart from the rest is the whole job.

{{SEC:problem}}. The problem

The six programs in this document, by name: rate+context, CoactDetect, LoCo, binned SCE, locust and SPIKE-synch. Who made each one is in the table in Section {{SEC:algorithms}}. Four of the six were built in this lab. locust is this project's modified version of the coordinated-event detector inside CICADA, a published analysis package from the Cossart lab (Denis and others, 2020); ours is fed the lab's own list of event times and makes its own decisions, so its results are ours and not CICADA's. SPIKE-synch is built on a published mathematical measure of how closely in time events in different neurons follow one another (Kreuz and others, 2015).

Figure {{NUM:fig_problem}} is a real recording. All six programs were given the same list of event times — the same raster, drawn at the bottom — and each program marked the moments where that program judged that neurons had acted together. One such mark is a call. A call is a claim by a program, not a fact about the neurons: it can be right or wrong, and nothing in the recording says which.

{{PROSE:problem_text}} A real recording has no answer key, so we cannot look up who is right.

Why it matters. A study of a drug compares calls before and during the drug. If the programs do not agree on what counts, a drug that only makes neurons busier can look as if it changes how they work together. So we also test the programs on simulated recordings, which we build ourselves with the coordinated events put in on purpose, so the answer is known (Section {{SEC:sim}}).

{{SEC:chance}}. Coordinated events happen by chance

Why is finding coordinated events hard? Because neurons have calcium events on their own all the time, and when they have them often, some of them land close together by luck alone.

To count that, cut the recording into short stretches of equal length — a bin is one of those stretches, 2 seconds long in Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}} — and count how many different neurons had an event inside each one. Most programs in this document work from a count like that, and they differ in how long they make their bins. In Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}}B nothing at all was put in, and yet {{T.busy_max}} neurons still had events inside the same 2-second bin.

So counting neurons is not enough. A program has to ask a harder question: does this coordinated event hold more neurons than chance alone would produce? To answer it, the program needs to know what chance looks like in this particular recording.

{{SEC:count}}. Why not just ask for a minimum number of events in an interval?

Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}} shows the difficulty: coordinated events happen by chance, and more often when neurons are busy. The simplest answer ignores chance: call a coordinated event whenever at least a set number of neurons have calcium events within a short interval of each other (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_rule}}). Each neuron counts once, however many events it has inside the interval. The number of neurons a rule asks for is its bar: the count that has to be reached before it calls a coordinated event. So why not use it?

Labs that study these same neurons use rules like it. Allan Herbison's lab, recording brain slices as this lab does, calls a "miniature synchronization event" whenever the event peaks of at least 2 neurons fall within 10 seconds of each other, and adds any further neuron whose peak falls within 10 seconds of the one before (Han and others, 2023). The same rule, still at 2 neurons, is used in the lab's slice papers through 2026 (Morris and Herbison, 2023; Eddleston and others, 2026). Aleisha Moore and coworkers, recording these neurons in living mice, called an episode a "synchronized episode" when every recorded neuron had a calcium event (Moore, Coolen and Lehman, 2022). This section asks how much chance such a rule lets through, with an interval of {{T.cnt_w}} seconds and with Herbison's rule. How the rule scores against the six programs comes later (Section {{SEC:scores}}), once the programs and the scoring have been explained.

Why an interval and not a bin

An even simpler version of the rule cuts the recording into fixed bins and counts the neurons in each. It has a flaw of its own: a coordinated event that falls across the edge between two bins is split, some of its neurons landing in one bin and the rest in the next, so neither bin may reach the bar. With a bar of {{T.edge_x}} neurons, fixed {{T.edge_bin}}-second bins found {{T.edge_bin_at}} of the 6-neuron events that fell close to an edge (within an eighth of a bin), against {{T.edge_bin_mid}} of those in the middle of a bin. A window that slides along with the events cannot split one, and found {{T.edge_int_at}} of them wherever they fell (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_edge}}). Herbison's rule slides in this way, which is one thing it gets right. Three of the six programs count in fixed bins. Two of them pay a smaller price for it: CoactDetect found {{T.edge_coact_at}} of the events at an edge against {{T.edge_coact_mid}} mid-bin, and LoCo {{T.edge_loco_at}} against {{T.edge_loco_mid}}. The third, binned SCE, uses 10-second bins and was not measured this way.

On real recordings, no single bar fits

Real recordings differ a great deal from one another. Across the lab's {{T.cnt_n_real}} untreated recordings, neurons had between {{T.cnt_rate_min}} and {{T.cnt_rate_max}} events per neuron per minute on average — between {{T.cnt_rate_p10}} and {{T.cnt_rate_p90}} in eight recordings out of ten — and a recording held {{T.cnt_cells_min}} to {{T.cnt_cells_max}} neurons. The bar that holds chance to less than once in 10 minutes climbs with how busy the neurons are, from {{T.cnt_need_min}} neurons to {{T.cnt_need_max}} (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_slices}}). A bar of {{T.cnt_one_bar}} neurons lets chance through in {{T.cnt_n_loose}} of the {{T.cnt_n_real}} recordings — in one of them {{T.cnt_loose_max}} times every 10 minutes — while in {{T.cnt_n_strict}} recordings a bar of 3 neurons would already have been enough.

That is the real trouble with a fixed bar. A drug that gives neurons more calcium events moves a recording to the right in Figure {{NUM:fig_count_slices}}. With a fixed bar, that recording then gets more coordinated events called, even if nothing about how the neurons work together has changed.

At the published rate, what does chance give?

Two of these papers show that drugs blocking glutamate signals between neurons cut the number of these events sharply (Han and others, 2023; Morris and Herbison, 2023), though the same drugs also cut how often each neuron has events, which by itself lowers the count chance gives. None compares the rule's count with what neurons would produce if their calcium events were independent, so we made the simplest version of that comparison (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_published}}). In the most recent paper, neurons had {{T.pub_rep_rate}} calcium events per neuron per hour, and the paper reports {{T.pub_rep_mse}} miniature synchronization events per neuron per hour, each joined by {{T.pub_rep_cells}} neurons ({{T.pub_rep_share}}% of the neurons in view) on average (Eddleston and others, 2026). Independent neurons at that rate, in a field of {{T.pub_field}} neurons — about the average those two numbers imply — meet the rule {{T.pub_chance_mse}} times per neuron per hour, with {{T.pub_chance_cells}} neurons in each.

"Per neuron per hour" can be read two ways, and the papers do not settle which. They divide the count by the number of neurons, and one of them describes the result as how often each neuron takes part in an event (Han and others, 2023). Read as a count divided by neurons, as Figure {{NUM:fig_count_published}} does, independent neurons give {{T.pub_chance_mse}} against the paper's {{T.pub_rep_mse}}. Read as how often each neuron takes part, they give {{T.pub_chance_part}} against {{T.pub_rep_mse}}. Either way, in a field of about {{T.pub_field}} neurons, independent neurons at the paper's rate meet the rule about as often as the paper reports or more, in smaller events. The field size matters: with 8 neurons in view chance falls a little below the reported rate, and with 29 chance events hold as many neurons as the reported ones (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_published}}). Real neurons do not have events at a steady rate, and none of the paper's data went into this, so it is not a verdict on its findings. It shows why a count needs a chance comparison beside it.

What this means

Asking for a minimum number of neurons within an interval is not wrong. What a fixed bar cannot do is tell a busy recording from a coordinated one, because the bar does not move when the neurons get busier, and on real recordings the bar that chance allows moves a long way. Counting in fixed bins instead of a sliding interval adds a second problem, the bin edge. The shifted copies of the next section let a program set its bar from the recording's own chance instead.

{{SEC:copies}}. Measuring chance with shifted copies

Four of the six programs measure chance the same basic way, the way Figure {{NUM:fig_chance_steps}} shows. The program makes shifted copies of the recording. To make one copy, it slides each neuron's whole row of events left or right by that neuron's own random amount; events pushed off one end come back in at the other end.

A shifted copy keeps everything about each neuron on its own: how many events that neuron has, and whether its events come in bursts or at a steady beat. What a shift destroys is the timing between neurons. So a coordinated event that survives in a copy is, as far as the shift can tell, one that chance can produce. Counting coordinated events in many copies (100 or 200, depending on the program) tells the program how high a count has to be before it means anything.

This is called a circular shift in the literature. "Shift" because each row slides; "circular" because the recording is treated as a loop, so what falls off one end comes back at the other. If you go looking for the papers behind these programs — they are listed at the end, under Where the ideas come from — that is the phrase to search for.

{{SEC:shuffle}}. Why shift, not shuffle

There is another common way to make a chance copy: shuffle the recording. A shuffle keeps the number of events each neuron has, but drops every one of them at a brand-new random time. That sounds fair, and it is not: a shuffle also destroys each neuron's own pattern, and then the chance bar comes out wrong. Figure {{NUM:fig_shift_shuffle}} shows the bar going wrong in both directions.

  • Neurons that have events in bursts (A). A shuffle breaks each burst apart and spreads the events out. Now more neurons land in any given bin by chance, so the bar comes out too high. A real burst of {{T.bursty_cells}} neurons, which shifted copies reach only {{T.bursty_shift}} of the time, is reached by {{T.bursty_shuffle}} of shuffled copies. The shuffle calls it chance and misses an event that was put in.
  • Neurons that have events at a steady beat (B). A shuffle lets a neuron's events pile up in some bins and leave others empty. Now fewer neurons reach any bin by chance, so the bar comes out too low. Here {{T.even_cells}} neurons land in one bin purely by luck. Shifted copies do that {{T.even_shift}} of the time, so the shift correctly says "chance". Shuffled copies do it only {{T.even_shuffle}} of the time, so the shuffle calls a coordinated event that is only chance.

The lab's own brief events are like the steady-beat neurons in one way: a neuron rarely has two of them close together (C). In {{T.n_baselines}} untreated recordings, a shuffle drops {{T.doubles_shuffle}} of every 1,000 events into a 2-second bin their own neuron had already filled, against {{T.doubles_real}} in the real recording and {{T.doubles_shift}} after a shift. As a result, shuffled copies say 6 or more neurons have events together in {{T.n6_shuffle}} of bins and shifted copies say {{T.n6_shift}}, while the recordings themselves have {{T.n6_real}}. A bar built from shuffles would sit lower still. That is why every program here that makes copies uses the shift instead.

The shift is better than a shuffle, and still not exact

It would be convenient if a shifted copy were a real recording with the coordination taken out and nothing else changed. It is not.

  • Real recordings hold more large coordinated events than the shift expects. Coordinated events of eight or more neurons happen about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often in the recordings as in their shifted copies. Some of that is probably slow rises and falls that many neurons share, which a shift breaks along with everything else.
  • A shifted copy carries traces of having been shifted. In this project's own tests, a learned program (one that works out its own rule from examples) trained to tell a real recording from a shifted copy of it can sometimes do so. The shift leaves a signature of its own.

So treat the shift as an idea that clearly helps and beats the obvious alternative, not as a solved problem. The bar of every program that makes copies rests on it, and that is a limit on the numbers that follow.

{{SEC:algorithms}}. The six algorithms

An algorithm is a fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that the computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs here are algorithms of this kind, and all six ask the same question, does this coordinated event hold more neurons than chance explains?, but the steps differ. The difference that matters most is where each program looks to learn what chance is:

The third column, where each program learns what chance looks like, decides how that program behaves when the neurons get busy.

CoactDetect, LoCo and rate+context work from the surrounding seconds. Each of them measures chance from the minute around the moment it is testing — by making shifted copies of that minute, or, for rate+context, by averaging it. When the neurons in that minute have events more often, those copies (or that average) give bigger counts, and the count a bin has to beat is computed from them, so it goes up as well. A coordinated event that chance produces inside a busy stretch therefore fails to beat it. The cost is that a small coordinated event that is really there has to beat that raised count too, and often cannot.

binned SCE and locust work from long stretches at once. Each of them measures chance one time, from copies of the entire stretch it was given (for binned SCE, each stretch the lab studies; for locust, the whole recording), and uses the single count that comes out of it everywhere. That count was fixed before the busy stretch arrived and does not move when it does, so every bin in the busy stretch can beat it, one after another.

{{SEC:sim}}. Simulated recordings, where we know the answer

Section {{SEC:problem}} left us with the problem that a real recording has no answer key. Here is the way around it. We simulate recordings: a program of our own writes out lists of event times in the same form the lab's event-finder produces, and we choose in advance when the neurons act together. Since we put those moments in, we know where every one of them is, and we can check what each program finds against what we put in.

The program that writes them is the simulator. Each simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons and lasts {{T.minutes}} minutes (Figure {{NUM:fig_simulator}}), and it is built in layers:

  • Background: each neuron has calcium events on its own, at its own rate. A few neurons are busy and many are nearly silent, as in real recordings. We made two versions: a quiet background (about {{T.quiet_per_hour}} events per neuron per hour, as set) and a busy background (about {{T.busy_per_hour}}). Those match the quieter and busier untreated recordings in the lab's data ({{T.real_p25_per_hour}} and {{T.real_p75_per_hour}} per hour).
  • Planted events: {{T.n_planted}} coordinated events that we put in on purpose. They come in three sizes, counted by how many of the {{T.bench_n_roi}} neurons join in: {{T.big_cells}} neurons, {{T.mid_cells}} neurons, or {{T.small_cells}} neurons. {{T.mid_cells}} neurons is the size that matters most: it is the typical size one analysis of the lab's real recordings estimated, though that estimate rests on choices the analyst made, and the stripes you can see in this document's real figures are often much larger. The {{T.small_cells}}-neuron size is the hardest case any program is asked to handle; three of the six will not call a coordinated event smaller than that at all. The neurons in one event start a little apart, not at exactly the same moment. Planted events are at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart.
  • A busy stretch: from minute {{T.hot_from}} to minute {{T.hot_to}}, every neuron gets about {{T.hot_per_hour}} extra random events per hour. Nothing is planted there, so any call there is a false alarm.
  • Decoys: {{T.n_decoys}} coordinated events built exactly like planted events, and deliberately left off the answer key.

Why we plant coordinated events and then refuse to count them

A program that marked every coordinated event it saw would find all {{T.n_planted}} planted events and look excellent, while telling us nothing about whether it can separate coordination from coincidence. Decoys make that cost something: they look exactly like planted events, so calling everything now costs a wrong answer for every decoy.

They also stand for something in real recordings. Some coordinated events there are coincidence — neurons that happen to have events together, or that share a slow drift in how often they have events, with nothing coordinating them. Decoys put coordinated events that look real but should not count into the test.

The price: a program that sees only event times cannot tell a decoy from a planted event. They are built the same way. So the test has a ceiling — the best possible overall score is about {{T.ceiling}} instead of 1.0, and no program in this document could reach the top no matter how good it was. That ceiling is deliberate. It is the part of the problem that no cleverness inside a program can solve, and keeping it in the test stops us from reading a high score as a solved problem.

What the simulator gets wrong, and why it is still wrong

Four things, and the reasons they have not been fixed are four different reasons.

  1. Its busiest neurons bunch their events together far more than real busy neurons do. Count each neuron's events in 30-second stretches and see how much those counts vary: 1 means as even as chance allows. Real busy neurons sit near {{T.bunch_real_busy}}; the simulator's sit near {{T.bunch_sim_busy}}. Why it stands: we know the cause — the simulator makes each neuron's rate rise and fall by the same relative swing, so the busier the neuron, the wilder the swing — and a better background model is open work. It is not a patch, because every score in this document was measured against this background, so changing it means running the whole test again.
  2. Its large coordinated events are rarer than real ones. In real recordings, coordinated events of 8 or more neurons happen about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often as in their shifted copies; the simulator does not reproduce that. Why it stands: this is the hard one. The simulator can only contain coordination we already know how to describe — so many neurons, starting within so many tenths of a second. Whatever produces the big coordinated events in real recordings has not been described yet. Building it into the simulator would mean already knowing the answer these programs are supposed to find.
  3. Every simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons, while real ones hold {{T.cnt_cells_min}} to {{T.cnt_cells_max}}, and several of the programs have rules that depend on how many neurons there are. Why it stands: this one is easy — the count is a setting. It has not been changed because changing it would make these scores incomparable with the ones before them. The right fix is to run the whole test at several neuron counts and report the spread, which costs computer time rather than new ideas.
  4. How far apart the neurons in a real coordinated event start is not well measured. Why it stands: the measurement does not yet work. Run it on a simulated recording built with a known spread and it does not return that spread, so there is nothing trustworthy to copy from the real recordings. A better estimator has to come first.

Underneath the second and fourth is the same limit: a simulator can only hold what we already know how to write down. The parts of a real recording we most want to test a program against are exactly the parts nobody can describe yet. Simulated recordings are still the only place an answer key exists, but a good score here is not a promise about real recordings, which is why Section {{SEC:real}} exists.

{{SEC:grading}}. How we grade a program

Grading compares each program's calls with the answer key. A call that lands within {{T.tol}} seconds of a planted event is a hit. Each planted event can be claimed by only one call, so a second call on the same event is a false alarm. A planted event with no call near it is a miss. Every other call is a false alarm, including a call on a decoy.

From those we get two numbers: the share of planted events a program found, and the share of its calls that were right. The overall score combines them into one number from 0 to 1. It is high only when both are high. (Its technical name is the F1 score.)

Calls inside the busy stretch are false alarms too, but they are kept out of the "right" share and counted on their own (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}, B). That stretch is busier than {{T.cnt_block_slower}} of the lab's {{T.cnt_n_real}} untreated recordings, and folded into the score it would mostly measure how hard the test was set. The cost is that the overall score cannot see a program that calls all the way through a busy stretch, so that has to be checked separately (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}, B, and Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, B).

How each program's main setting was chosen. Most programs have one main setting that raises or lowers their bar. On {{T.retune_date}} all six went through the same search procedure. Each program's main setting was tried at a range of values on {{T.n_retune}} simulated recordings of each background. The one with the best overall score across both backgrounds won, but it replaced the value already stored only if its gain was clearly larger than chance variation between recordings (a 95% range for the gain that excludes zero). The value moved for {{T.retune_moved}}; {{T.retune_kept}} kept their stored values, because nothing beat them by more than that or, for SPIKE-synch, because every value that scored better broke its false-alarm limit (below).

A value was also allowed only if the program stayed under its own two limits on false alarms, and those limits differ from program to program. Each was set from how often that program called where nothing was planted before the search, with some room added, so it stops a program from getting worse rather than holding all six to one standard. In the busy stretch the limit runs from {{T.lim_probe_lo}} call per minute ({{T.lim_probe_lo_who}}) to {{T.lim_probe_hi}} ({{T.lim_probe_hi_who}}); on a whole recording with nothing planted, from {{T.lim_null_lo}} call per hour ({{T.lim_null_lo_who}}) to {{T.lim_null_hi}} ({{T.lim_null_hi_who}}). For binned SCE the limit decided the result: a much looser bar (1 bin in {{T.sce_loose_1in}}) scored {{T.sce_loose_f1}} on average, against {{T.sce_stored_f1}} at the stored 1 in {{T.sce_stored_1in}}, but made {{T.sce_loose_null}} calls an hour on the empty recording, where its limit is {{T.sce_null_limit}} and the stored value makes {{T.sce_stored_null}}. Whether that trade is worth making is an open decision for the project.

Each program then runs at that one stored value, the same on every recording, real or simulated. Every score in this document was measured on {{T.n_recordings_scored}} other simulated recordings that the search never saw, so no score comes from the recordings its setting was picked on.

What the search did not do. It searched only each program's main setting; the others keep the values the programs were written with. Every value was chosen against the simulator, whose faults Section {{SEC:sim}} lists, and real recordings have no answer key, so nothing can be tuned on them at all. CoactDetect and LoCo count neurons in fixed bins, which can split a coordinated event across a bin edge (Section {{SEC:count}}); versions of both that slide instead of stepping are being tested, and are not the ones in this document.

{{SEC:scores}}. Scores on the simulated recordings

On the quiet background CoactDetect ({{T.f1q_coact}}) and LoCo ({{T.f1q_loco}}) scored highest, then rate+context ({{T.f1q_rate}}) (Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}). The best possible was about {{T.ceiling}}. With this many test recordings we cannot say that CoactDetect is truly better than LoCo. binned SCE ({{T.f1q_sce}}) and locust ({{T.f1q_cicada}}) scored lower, and SPIKE-synch ({{T.f1q_sync}}) lowest. On the busy background every score drops a little: CoactDetect {{T.f1b_coact}}, LoCo {{T.f1b_loco}}, rate+context {{T.f1b_rate}}, binned SCE {{T.f1b_sce}}, locust {{T.f1b_cicada}} and SPIKE-synch {{T.f1b_sync}}.

Busy stretches are where the programs split apart (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}). The {{T.n_recordings_scored}} test recordings never put a coordinated event inside the busy stretch, so a program that goes quiet there looks good even if it has gone blind. To find out which, we made {{T.block_recordings}} new recordings with coordinated events planted inside the busy stretch.

  • CoactDetect and LoCo, which judge chance from nearby time, stay quiet in the busy stretch, but they also miss most planted events there: CoactDetect found {{T.out_coact}} of the {{T.mid_cells}}-neuron events outside it and {{T.in_coact}} inside, LoCo {{T.out_loco}} and {{T.in_loco}}.
  • locust and binned SCE, which judge chance from long stretches, keep calling in the busy stretch: about {{T.fa_cicada}} and {{T.fa_sce}} false alarms per minute. binned SCE calls all the way through it, so its calls there tell us nothing.
  • rate+context did both jobs fairly well at this level of busyness ({{T.in_rate}} found inside, {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms per minute). When we made the stretch three times busier, none of the programs held up: CoactDetect found {{T.in3_coact}} of the planted events there and LoCo {{T.in3_loco}}, and rate+context found {{T.in3_rate}} only by calling {{T.fa3_rate}} times a minute where nothing was planted.

How the interval rule scores

With the right bar, the rule scores about as well as the best programs. With a bar of {{T.cnt_best_quiet}} neurons it scored {{T.cnt_f1_best_quiet}} on the quiet background; CoactDetect scored {{T.cnt_coact_f1_quiet}} on the same recordings (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, A). That result flatters the rule a little, because its bar was picked on these very recordings, with the answers in hand.

But the right bar depends on the recording. On the busy background the best bar is {{T.cnt_best_busy}} neurons. Use the quiet background's bar of {{T.cnt_best_quiet}} there and the score falls to {{T.cnt_quiet_bar_on_busy}}. The one bar that does best on both backgrounds together, {{T.cnt_one_bar}} neurons, scores {{T.cnt_f1_one_quiet}} on the quiet background and {{T.cnt_f1_one_busy}} on the busy one.

And the bar that scores best calls most of the busy stretch, where nothing was planted (Figure {{NUM:fig_count_bar}}, B). At its best bar, the rule called {{T.cnt_empty_best_quiet}} of that stretch on the quiet background and {{T.cnt_empty_best_busy}} on the busy one. On the busy background, CoactDetect called {{T.cnt_coact_empty_busy}} of the stretch and LoCo {{T.cnt_loco_empty_busy}}. A 10-second interval did not escape this: its best bars ({{T.cnt10_best_quiet}} neurons on the quiet background, {{T.cnt10_best_busy}} on the busy one) scored {{T.cnt10_f1_quiet}} and {{T.cnt10_f1_busy}}, and called at least {{T.cnt10_empty}} of the stretch. Herbison's rule, as published but applied to our event lists (without its step that ignores a neuron's own repeat events within 10 seconds), called at least {{T.herb_empty_busy}} of it and scored {{T.herb_f1_quiet}} on the quiet background and {{T.herb_f1_busy}} on the busy one — though our simulated recordings hold {{T.n_cells_sim}} neurons, more than the 8 to 29 in view in that lab's slices, and a rule built for fewer neurons is reached more easily with more.

The overall score did not show this, because calls inside the busy stretch are kept out of it and counted on their own (Section {{SEC:grading}}). Two of the six programs have the same weakness: binned SCE, which counts neurons in bins and sets one bar for each long stretch it studies, called {{T.cnt_sce_empty_busy}} of the stretch, and locust called {{T.cnt_cicada_empty_busy}}. CoactDetect and LoCo avoid it by setting their bar from the minutes around each moment, so a busy stretch raises its own bar. That is what the extra work buys.

{{SEC:real}}. Detection on real recordings

A real recording has no answer key. The figures in this section show where each program made calls. No figure here can show whether a call is right.

Learned models are left out. The project is also building learned models, programs that work out their own rule from examples; they are not drawn or judged here, and a separate document covers them.

The lab tests two drugs. TTX (tetrodotoxin) blocks the fast electrical signals most neurons use. Senktide switches on a sensor these neurons carry, the neurokinin 3 receptor (Navarro and others, 2009). Many recordings end with high potassium, which makes healthy neurons produce calcium events, so a neuron with no events through it was probably never going to show us anything. One might expect TTX to stop all coordination between neurons, which would give us a ready-made answer key, and in the Herbison lab's slices TTX stopped all calcium events (Han and others, 2023). In this lab's recordings it does not: calcium events continue under TTX, and so do coordinated events. So a TTX recording cannot serve as a "no coordination" test here.

The lab marks analysis windows: the stretches of each recording it studies. Four of the six programs run only inside those windows. LoCo and locust run on the whole recording.

{{PROSE:real_overview}}

{{SEC:eye}}. Where your eye and the programs disagree

Two things stand out in Figures {{NUM:real_ttx_brief}} to {{NUM:real_senk_long}}. Sharp stripes that anyone can see, and whether the programs call them; and calls that sit where the raster shows nothing special. Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}} looks closely at both.

The second half of that rule keeps the ▼ marks off busy stretches, where coordinated events joined by a quarter of the neurons often happen by chance alone (Section {{SEC:chance}}). It has a cost: inside a busy stretch the rule can hardly mark anything, so a call there is unjudged, not shown to be wrong. The rule is our own and no person has rated its marks. Its numbers (a quarter of the neurons, 1 second, twice the busiest seconds of 2 minutes) are our choice and were not tested against anyone's marks, and it is close in spirit to rate+context's own test, so rate+context agreeing with it is partly built in.

{{PROSE:eye_findings}}

{{SEC:means}}. What this means

  • No program is shown to be best. On simulated recordings CoactDetect and LoCo score highest and cannot be told apart, and both miss most planted coordinated events inside busy stretches, where rate+context finds most of them at the cost of about {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms a minute.
  • Busy neurons change every program's answer. Some go blind in busy stretches, some call all through them. A drug that makes neurons busier can change the calls even if coordination between neurons has not changed. Any study of a drug has to separate the two.
  • A call means "unusual for this recording", not "big". In a quiet recording, a coordinated event of 3 neurons can be rare enough to call. That is correct for the math and unconvincing to the eye.
    • {{PROSE:meaning_real}}

What could be wrong here

  • Every score comes from simulated recordings, and the simulator is imperfect (Section {{SEC:sim}}).
  • The bars of the four programs that make copies come from shifted copies, and a shifted copy is not a perfect stand-in for a recording with the coordination removed (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
  • The chance levels in the programs' settings ("1 in 10,000" and so on) are what the settings aim for, per bin. Each program checks thousands of bins, so a few calls by chance happen in every recording.
  • The settings were searched and scored on simulated brief events only; the same settings are used on long events.
  • Each program's own false-alarm limits kept it from getting worse during the settings search; they are not one standard for all six (Section {{SEC:grading}}).
  • We ran no statistical test between the top programs, and the decoys cap every score.
  • The "clear stripe" rule in Section {{SEC:eye}} is our own. A person may see stripes it misses, or miss some it counts.
  • The real recordings shown are one per group of mice, per drug. That is too few to say how common any of this is.
  • Planted events are always at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart. That hides a known weakness of the programs that judge chance from nearby time: two coordinated events close together can raise each other's bar, and a single event sits inside its own nearby minute and raises its own bar a little.

Word list

Algorithm
A fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that a computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs in this document are algorithms.
Analysis window
A stretch of a recording the lab marks for study. Some programs look only inside these windows.
Bar
The level a program's count must pass before it makes a call.
Bin
One of the short, equal stretches of time a recording is cut into before counting — 1, 2 or 10 seconds long, depending on the program.
Brief events, long events
The lab's event-finding program keeps short calcium events and much longer ones in two separate lists. Each list is studied on its own.
Calcium event
An increase followed by a decrease in one neuron's brightness: calcium inside that neuron went up and then cleared again. It is what we observe; what caused the rise is a separate question.
Call
One moment a program marks as a coordinated event. A call is that program's claim, not a fact about the neurons.
Clear stripe
Our rule for a coordinated event anyone can see on a raster (Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}}).
Coordinated event
A moment when several neurons have calcium events at about the same time. Chance alone produces some; the programs ask which ones hold more neurons than chance explains.
Decoy
A coordinated event put into a simulated recording the same way a planted event is, but left off the answer key.
False alarm
A call where there was no planted event.
GCaMP6f
The fluorescent protein these neurons build themselves, from a gene the mice carry. It gives off more light when calcium is around it, which is what makes a rise in calcium visible under the microscope.
High potassium
A solution that makes healthy neurons produce calcium events, often used at the end of a recording as a check that a quiet neuron could have shown something.
Miniature synchronization event (mSE)
The name Allan Herbison's lab uses for a call of its interval rule: at least 2 neurons whose event peaks fall within 10 seconds of each other (Section {{SEC:count}}).
Overall score (F1 score)
One number from 0 to 1 that is high only when a program both finds most planted events and makes few false alarms.
Planted event
A coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose, at a time we chose.
Raster
A picture of a recording: one row per neuron, one tick per event.
Senktide
A drug that switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor, a sensor these neurons carry.
Shifted copy (a circular shift, in the research literature)
A copy of a recording in which each neuron's whole row of events is slid by that neuron's own random amount, with events pushed off one end coming back at the other. A shift keeps each neuron's own pattern and breaks only the timing between neurons.
Shuffled copy
A copy in which every event is moved to a new random time. A shuffle changes each neuron's own pattern as well, which is why it gets chance wrong (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
Simulate, simulator
To produce a recording ourselves instead of measuring one, choosing in advance when the neurons act together. The simulator is the program that writes those recordings.
TTX (tetrodotoxin)
A drug that blocks the fast electrical signals most neurons use.
