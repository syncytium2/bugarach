Finding cells that act together

How six computer programs decide when many brain cells light up at the same time, how we tested them, and why they sometimes disagree with your own eyes.

The short version

  • The lab records brain cells through a microscope with a digital camera. A cell brightens whenever the calcium inside it goes up. We want to find the moments when many cells brighten together. Computer programs have to find those moments, because there are far too many recordings to go through by hand.
  • Cells brighten on their own as well, so some of them line up just by chance. Every program has to separate real teamwork from chance lineups, and each program uses a different set of steps to do that.
  • A real recording has no answer key: nobody knows for certain which moments are real. So we also simulate recordings — we produce them ourselves, choosing in advance when the cells act together — and check what each program finds against what we put in.
  • On simulated recordings, several programs score about the same. None of them is clearly best.

{{SEC:records}}. What the lab records

Figure {{NUM:fig_orient}} is the picture everything else in this document is built on: the stripes are what we are trying to find. Here is where it comes from.

The lab studies a small group of brain cells called KNDy neurons. These cells sit in the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the body's reproductive system (Lehman and others, 2010). The lab keeps thin slices of mouse brain alive in a dish and records them through a microscope with a digital camera, about ten pictures a second.

What makes a cell visible? These mice carry an extra gene, so the cells build a glowing protein of their own called GCaMP6f. Nothing is added to them; they make it themselves. The protein is dim on its own and bright when calcium is around it. So when the amount of calcium inside a cell goes up, that cell brightens for a moment and then fades again as the calcium clears.

That brief brightening is a calcium event, and it is the whole of what we observe. It says the calcium inside that cell went up. It does not say why, and this document does not assume why — which matters later, because one of the drugs in Section {{SEC:real}} blocks the electrical signals that are usually behind a calcium rise, and the events carry on regardless.

A separate program goes through the pictures, finds each brightening and writes down the moment it happened. That program keeps two lists: one for brief events and one for much longer events.

Those lists of moments are all the programs in this document ever see. None of them looks at the pictures. We draw a list as a raster, the picture above: one row per cell, one tick per event. A short stripe in a raster means many cells lit up at nearly the same moment. That is a coordinated event, and finding coordinated events is the whole job.

{{SEC:problem}}. The problem

The six programs in this document, by name: rate+context, CoactDetect, LoCo, binned SCE, locust and SPIKE-synch. Who made each one is in the table in Section {{SEC:algorithms}}. Four of the six were built in this lab. locust is this project's modified version of the coordinated-event detector inside CICADA, a published analysis package from the Cossart lab (Denis and others, 2020); ours is fed the lab's own list of event times and uses its own bar, so its results are ours and not CICADA's. SPIKE-synch is built on a published measure of how close events in different cells are in time (Kreuz and others, 2015).

Figure {{NUM:fig_problem}} is a real recording. All six programs were given the same list of event times — the same raster, drawn at the bottom — and each program marked the moments where that program judged that cells had acted together. One such mark is a call. A call is a claim by a program, not a fact about the cells: it can be right or wrong, and nothing in the recording says which.

{{PROSE:problem_text}} A real recording has no answer key, so we cannot look up who is right. This document explains how each program decides, how we tested the programs on recordings whose answer we do know, and where the programs and a person's eye disagree.

{{SEC:chance}}. Lineups happen by chance

Why is finding coordinated events hard? Because cells brighten on their own all the time, and when they do it often, some of them land close together by luck alone.

To count that, cut the recording into short stretches of equal length — a bin is one of those stretches, 2 seconds long in Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}} — and count how many different cells brightened inside each one. Most programs in this document work from a count like that, and they differ in how long they make their bins. In Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}}B nothing at all was put in, and yet {{T.busy_max}} cells still brightened inside the same 2-second bin.

So counting cells is not enough. A program has to ask a harder question: are more cells lining up right now than chance alone would produce? To answer it, the program needs to know what chance looks like in this particular recording.

{{SEC:copies}}. Measuring chance with shifted copies

Four of the six programs measure chance the same basic way, the way Figure {{NUM:fig_chance_steps}} shows. The program makes shifted copies of the recording. To make one copy, it slides each cell's whole row of events left or right by that cell's own random amount; events pushed off one end come back in at the other end.

A shifted copy keeps everything about each cell on its own: how often that cell brightens, and whether its events come in bursts or at a steady beat. What a shift destroys is the timing between cells. So a lineup that survives in a copy is, as far as the shift can tell, a lineup that chance can produce. Counting lineups in a hundred copies tells the program how high a real count has to be before it means anything. Section {{SEC:shuffle}} is about why this particular way of making copies, and about where it falls short.

This is called a circular shift in the literature. "Shift" because each row slides; "circular" because the recording is treated as a loop, so what falls off one end comes back at the other. If you go looking for the papers behind these programs — they are listed at the end, under Where the ideas come from — that is the phrase to search for.

{{SEC:shuffle}}. Why shift, not shuffle

There is another common way to make a chance copy: shuffle the recording. A shuffle keeps the number of events each cell has, but drops every one of them at a brand-new random time. That sounds fair, and it is not: a shuffle also destroys each cell's own pattern, and then the chance bar comes out wrong. Figure {{NUM:fig_shift_shuffle}} shows the bar going wrong in both directions.

  • Cells that fire in bursts (A). A shuffle breaks each burst apart and spreads the events out. Now more cells land in any given bin by chance, so the bar comes out too high. A real burst of {{T.bursty_cells}} cells, which shifted copies reach only {{T.bursty_shift}} of the time, is reached by {{T.bursty_shuffle}} of shuffled copies. The shuffle calls it chance and misses a real event.
  • Cells that fire at a steady beat (B). A shuffle lets a cell's events pile up in some bins and leave others empty. Now fewer cells reach any bin by chance, so the bar comes out too low. Here {{T.even_cells}} cells land in one bin purely by luck. Shifted copies do that {{T.even_shift}} of the time, so the shift correctly says "chance". Shuffled copies do it only {{T.even_shuffle}} of the time, so the shuffle calls a chance lineup.

The lab's own brief events behave like the steady-beat cells (C). In {{T.n_baselines}} untreated recordings, a shuffle drops {{T.doubles_shuffle}} of every 1,000 events into a bin their own cell had already filled, against {{T.doubles_real}} in the real recording and {{T.doubles_shift}} after a shift. As a result, shuffled copies say 6 or more cells line up in {{T.n6_shuffle}} of bins, and shifted copies say {{T.n6_shift}}. A bar built from shuffles would sit too low. That is why every program here that makes copies uses the shift instead.

The shift is the better choice, not the right answer

It would be convenient if a shifted copy were a real recording with the coordination taken out and nothing else changed. It is not, and it is worth saying so plainly.

  • Real recordings line up more than the shift expects, even where nothing is coordinated. Eight or more cells line up about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often in the recordings as in their shifted copies. Some of that is probably slow rises and falls that many cells share, which a shift breaks along with everything else.
  • A shifted copy carries traces of having been shifted. In this project's own tests, a program trained to tell a real recording from a shifted copy of it can sometimes do so. The shift leaves a signature of its own, so a copy is not a silent, invisible version of the recording.

So treat the shift as what it is: an idea that clearly helps, and that beats the obvious alternative, rather than a solved problem. Every bar in this document rests on it, and that is a limit on every number that follows.

{{SEC:algorithms}}. The six algorithms

An algorithm is a fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that the computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs here are algorithms of this kind, and all six ask the same question, are more cells lining up right now than chance explains?, but the steps differ. The difference that matters most is where each program looks to learn what chance is:

CoactDetectcells lit in each 2-second binshifted copies of the nearby minuteR. DeFazio's design, built for this project
LoCocells lit in each 1-second binshifted copies of the minute before and the minute afterR. DeFazio's design, built for this project
binned SCEcells lit in each 10-second binshifted copies of the whole stretch being studiedthe rule published by Cossart, Aronov and Yuste (2003); written here
locustcells "switched on" after each of their eventsshifted copies of the whole recordingthe coordinated-event stage of CICADA, from the Cossart lab, modified here
SPIKE-synchhow close each event is to events in other cellsno copies: each cell's own gaps between eventsKreuz and colleagues' published measure, with R. DeFazio's peak-finding step added to turn its scores into calls

That last column decides how a program behaves when the cells get busy, so it is worth following through slowly.

CoactDetect, LoCo and rate+context work from the surrounding seconds. Each of them measures chance from the minute around the moment it is testing — by making shifted copies of that minute, or, for rate+context, by averaging it. When the cells in that minute brighten more often, those copies produce bigger counts, and the count a bin has to beat is computed from them, so it goes up as well. A chance lineup inside a busy stretch therefore fails to beat it. The cost is that a small real coordinated event in the same stretch has to beat that raised count too, and often cannot.

binned SCE and locust work from the whole recording at once. Each of them measures chance one time, from copies of the entire stretch it was given, and uses the single count that comes out of it everywhere. That count was fixed before the busy stretch arrived and does not move when it does, so every bin in the busy stretch can beat it, one after another.

{{SEC:sim}}. Simulated recordings, where we know the answer

Section {{SEC:problem}} left us with the problem that a real recording has no answer key. Here is the way around it. We simulate recordings: a program of our own writes out lists of event times in the same form the lab's event-finder produces, and we choose in advance when the cells act together. Since we put those moments in, we know where every one of them is, and we can check what each program finds against what we put in.

The program that writes them is the simulator. Each simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells and lasts {{T.minutes}} minutes (Figure {{NUM:fig_simulator}}), and it is built in layers:

  • Background: each cell fires on its own, at its own rate. A few cells are busy and many are nearly silent, as in real recordings. We made two versions: a quiet background (about {{T.quiet_per_hour}} events per cell per hour) and a busy background (about {{T.busy_per_hour}}). Those match the quieter and busier untreated recordings in the lab's data ({{T.real_p25_per_hour}} and {{T.real_p75_per_hour}} per hour).
  • Planted events: {{T.n_planted}} coordinated events that we put in on purpose, joined by 30%, 18% or 10% of the cells. The cells in one event start a little apart, not at exactly the same moment. Planted events are at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart.
  • A busy stretch: from minute {{T.hot_from}} to minute {{T.hot_to}}, every cell gets about {{T.hot_per_hour}} extra random events per hour. Nothing is planted there, so any call there is a false alarm.
  • Decoys: {{T.n_decoys}} lineups built exactly like planted events, and deliberately left off the answer key.

Why we plant lineups and then refuse to count them

Decoys are the part of the recipe that surprises people, so here is the reasoning.

The easiest way to score well on a test is to call everything. A program that marked every lineup it saw would find all {{T.n_planted}} planted events and look excellent, while telling us nothing about whether it can separate coordination from coincidence. Decoys take that route away: they look exactly like the real thing, so calling everything now costs as many wrong answers as it buys right ones.

They also stand for something real. In a live recording some lineups genuinely are coincidence — cells that happen to go off together, or that share a slow drift in how often they brighten, with nothing coordinating them. A person reading the raster would not count those. Decoys put that same population of "real lineups that should not count" into the test.

The price is worth stating plainly: a program that sees only event times cannot tell a decoy from a planted event. They are built the same way. So the test has a ceiling — the best possible overall score is about {{T.ceiling}} instead of 1.0, and no program in this document could reach the top no matter how good it was. That ceiling is deliberate. It is the part of the problem that no cleverness inside a program can solve, and keeping it in the test stops us from reading a high score as a solved problem.

What the simulator gets wrong. Its busiest cells bunch their events together far more than real busy cells do. Its large lineups are rarer than real ones. Every simulated recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells, while real recordings run from about half that to more. And how far apart the cells in a real coordinated event start is not well measured. So a good score on simulated recordings does not promise a good score on real ones.

{{SEC:grading}}. How we grade a program

Grading compares each program's calls with the answer key. A call that lands within {{T.tol}} seconds of a planted event is a hit. A planted event with no call near it is a miss. Every other call is a false alarm, including a call on a decoy.

From those we get two numbers: the share of planted events a program found, and the share of its calls that were right. The overall score combines them into one number from 0 to 1. It is high only when both are high. (Its technical name is the F1 score.)

Most programs have one main setting that raises or lowers their bar. To choose it fairly, we made {{T.n_recordings_scored}} test recordings and split them into {{T.n_tests}} groups of {{T.per_test}}. For each group, we picked the best setting using the other {{T.n_choose}} recordings, then scored the program on the {{T.per_test}} it had never seen.

{{SEC:scores}}. Scores on the simulated recordings

On the quiet background, three programs scored about the same (Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}): CoactDetect {{T.f1q_coact}}, LoCo {{T.f1q_loco}} and rate+context {{T.f1q_rate}}. The best possible was about {{T.ceiling}}. With this many test recordings we cannot say that any one of them is truly better than the others. binned SCE looks worse ({{T.f1q_sce}}), but most of that comes from how its calls are timed: when we score it over its whole 10-second bins, it reaches {{T.sce_fair_quiet}}. locust ({{T.f1q_cicada}}) and SPIKE-synch ({{T.f1q_sync}}) scored lower.

Busy stretches are where the programs split apart (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}). The recordings above never put a coordinated event inside the busy stretch, so a program that goes quiet there looks good even if it has gone blind. To find out which, we made {{T.block_recordings}} new recordings with coordinated events planted inside the busy stretch.

  • CoactDetect and LoCo, which judge chance from nearby time, stay quiet in the busy stretch, but they also miss most real events there: CoactDetect found {{T.out_coact}} of mid-sized events outside it and {{T.in_coact}} inside.
  • locust and binned SCE, which judge chance from the whole recording, keep calling in the busy stretch: about {{T.fa_cicada}} and {{T.fa_sce}} false alarms per minute. binned SCE calls all the way through it, so its calls there tell us nothing.
  • rate+context did both jobs fairly well at this level of busyness ({{T.in_rate}} found inside, {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms per minute). When we made the stretch three times busier, none of the programs held up.

{{SEC:real}}. The programs on real recordings

A real recording has no answer key. The figures in this section show where each program made calls. No figure here can show whether a call is right.

The lab tests two drugs. TTX (tetrodotoxin) blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve cells use. Senktide switches on a sensor these cells carry, the neurokinin 3 receptor (Navarro and others, 2009). Many recordings end with high potassium, which makes healthy cells produce calcium events, so a cell that stays dark through it was probably never going to show us anything. One might expect TTX to stop all teamwork between cells, which would give us a ready-made answer key. In this lab's recordings TTX does not stop it: events and lineups continue under the drug. So a TTX recording cannot serve as a "no coordination" test.

The lab marks analysis windows: the stretches of each recording it studies. Four of the six programs run only inside those windows. LoCo and locust run on the whole recording.

{{SEC:eye}}. Where your eye and the programs disagree

Two things stand out in Figures {{NUM:real_ttx_brief}} to {{NUM:real_senk_long}}. Sharp stripes that anyone can see are sometimes missed by most programs, and some calls sit where the raster shows nothing special. Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}} looks closely at both.

The second half of that rule is what makes the ▼ marks match what a person would point at. Inside a busy stretch, a quarter of the cells often line up by chance alone (Section {{SEC:chance}}), and nobody reading the raster would call those stripes.

{{SEC:means}}. What this means

  • No program is shown to be best. On simulated recordings, five score about the same.
  • Busy cells change every program's answer. Some go blind in busy stretches, some call all through them. A drug that makes cells busier can change the calls even if teamwork between cells has not changed. Any study of a drug has to separate the two.
  • A call means "unusual for this recording", not "big". In a quiet recording, 3 cells lining up can be rare enough to call. That is correct for the math and unconvincing to the eye.
    • {{PROSE:meaning_real}}

What could be wrong here

  • Every score comes from simulated recordings, and the simulator is imperfect (Section {{SEC:sim}}).
  • Every bar in this document comes from shifted copies, and a shifted copy is not a perfect stand-in for a recording with the coordination removed (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
  • We ran no statistical test between the top programs, and the decoys cap every score.
  • The "clear stripe" rule in Section {{SEC:eye}} is our own. A person may see stripes it misses, or miss some it counts.
  • The real recordings shown are one per group of mice, per drug. That is too few to say how common any of this is.
  • Planted events are always at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart. That hides a known weakness of the programs that judge chance from nearby time: two real events close together can raise each other's bar.

Word list

Algorithm
A fixed list of steps, written out by a person, that a computer carries out in the same order every time. All six programs in this document are algorithms.
Analysis window
A stretch of a recording the lab marks for study. Some programs look only inside these windows.
Bar
The level a program's count must pass before it makes a call.
Bin
One of the short, equal stretches of time a recording is cut into before counting — 1, 2 or 10 seconds long, depending on the program.
Brief events, long events
The lab's event-finding program keeps short brightenings and much longer ones in two separate lists. Each list is studied on its own.
Calcium event
A short brightening of one cell in the recording, which means calcium entered that cell and then cleared again. It is what we observe; what caused the rise is a separate question.
GCaMP6f
The glowing protein these cells build themselves, from a gene the mice carry. It is dim on its own and bright when calcium is around it, which is what makes a rise in calcium visible under the microscope.
Call
One moment a program marks as a coordinated event. A call is that program's claim, not a fact about the cells.
Clear stripe
Our rule for a lineup anyone can see on a raster (Figure {{NUM:fig_eye}}).
Coordinated event
A moment when many cells brighten together, more than chance explains.
Decoy
A lineup put into a simulated recording the same way a planted event is, but left off the answer key.
False alarm
A call where there was no planted event.
High potassium
A solution that makes healthy cells produce calcium events, often used at the end of a recording as a check that a quiet cell could have shown something.
Overall score (F1 score)
One number from 0 to 1 that is high only when a program both finds most planted events and makes few false alarms.
Planted event
A coordinated event we put into a simulated recording on purpose, at a time we chose.
Raster
A picture of a recording: one row per cell, one tick per event.
Senktide
A drug that switches on the neurokinin 3 receptor, a sensor these cells carry.
Shifted copy (a circular shift, in the research literature)
A copy of a recording in which each cell's whole row of events is slid by that cell's own random amount, with events pushed off one end coming back at the other. A shift keeps each cell's own pattern and breaks only the timing between cells.
Shuffled copy
A copy in which every event is moved to a new random time. A shuffle changes each cell's own pattern as well, which is why it gets chance wrong (Section {{SEC:shuffle}}).
Simulate, simulator
To produce a recording ourselves instead of measuring one, choosing in advance when the cells act together. The simulator is the program that writes those recordings.
TTX (tetrodotoxin)
A drug that blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve cells use.
