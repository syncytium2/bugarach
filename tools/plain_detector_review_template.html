Finding cells that act together

How ten computer programs decide when many brain cells light up at the same time, how we tested them, and why they sometimes disagree with your own eyes.

The short version

  • The lab films brain cells that flash when they are active. We want to find the moments when many cells flash together. We use computer programs to find them, because there are far too many recordings to check by hand.
  • Cells also flash on their own, so some flashes line up just by chance. Every program has to tell real teamwork from chance lineups. Each one does it with a different recipe.
  • On made-up recordings where we know the right answer, several programs do about equally well. None of them is clearly best.

1. What the lab records

The lab studies a small group of brain cells called KNDy neurons. They sit in the part of the brain that controls hormones, and they help run the body's reproductive system (Lehman and others, 2010). The lab keeps thin slices of mouse brain alive in a dish and films them under a microscope.

Each cell holds a dye that glows brighter when the cell is active. A short brightening is called a calcium event. A program finds each event and writes down the moment it happened. It keeps two lists: one for brief events and one for much longer events.

We draw those lists as a raster (Figure {{NUM:fig_raster}}). Each row is one cell and each tick is one event. When many cells light up at the same moment, their ticks stack into a short up-and-down stripe. That is a coordinated event, and finding those is the whole job.

2. The problem

Figure {{NUM:fig_problem}} shows a real recording. We gave the same list of events to six programs. Each program marks the moments it thinks are coordinated events. We call each mark a call.

{{PROSE:problem_text}} A real recording has no answer key, so we cannot simply look up who is right. This document explains how each program decides and how we tested them. It also looks closely at the places where the programs and a person's eye disagree.

3. Lineups happen by chance

Why is this hard? Cells light up on their own all the time. When cells are busy, some of their events will line up just by luck. Figure {{NUM:fig_chance}} shows this. On the left, quiet cells hardly ever line up, so the one real event stands out. On the right, the cells are busy and nothing real was added. Even so, up to {{T.busy_max}} cells land in the same 2-second piece by chance.

So a program cannot just count cells. It must ask a better question: are more cells lining up right now than chance would explain? To answer that, it needs to know what chance looks like.

4. Measuring chance with shifted copies

Four of the six programs measure chance the same basic way. They make shifted copies of the recording. To make a copy, the program slides each cell's whole row of events left or right by its own random amount. Events pushed off one end come back in at the other end.

A shifted copy keeps everything about each cell: how often it fires, and whether its events come in bursts or at a steady beat. The only thing it breaks is the timing between cells. So any lineup left in a copy must be a chance lineup. Figure {{NUM:fig_chance_steps}} walks through the steps.

5. Why shift, not shuffle

There is another common way to make a chance copy: shuffle it. A shuffle keeps how many events each cell has, but drops every event at a brand-new random time. That sounds fair, but it changes each cell's own pattern, and that makes the chance bar wrong. Figure {{NUM:fig_shift_shuffle}} shows it going wrong in both directions.

  • Cells that fire in bursts (A). A shuffle breaks each burst apart and spreads the events out. Now more cells land in any given piece by chance, so the bar comes out too high. A real burst of {{T.bursty_cells}} cells, which shifted copies reach only {{T.bursty_shift}} of the time, is reached by {{T.bursty_shuffle}} of shuffled copies. The shuffle calls it chance and misses a real event.
  • Cells that fire at a steady beat (B). A shuffle lets a cell's events pile up in some pieces and leave others empty. Now fewer cells reach any piece by chance, so the bar comes out too low. Here {{T.even_cells}} cells land in one piece purely by luck. Shifted copies do that {{T.even_shift}} of the time, so the shift correctly says "chance". Shuffled copies do it only {{T.even_shuffle}} of the time, so the shuffle calls a chance lineup.

The lab's own brief events behave like the steady-beat cells (C). In {{T.n_baselines}} untreated recordings, a shuffle drops {{T.doubles_shuffle}} of every 1,000 events into a piece their own cell had already filled, against {{T.doubles_real}} in the real recording and {{T.doubles_shift}} after a shift. As a result, shuffled copies say 6 or more cells line up in {{T.n6_shuffle}} of pieces, and shifted copies say {{T.n6_shift}}. A bar built from shuffles would sit too low. That is why every program here that makes copies uses the shift.

Even the shift is not perfect. Real recordings have more very large lineups than shifted copies predict: 8 or more cells line up about {{T.real_over_shift_8}} times as often as the copies say. Part of that may be slow ups and downs that many cells share, which shifting also breaks.

6. The six algorithms

An algorithm is a recipe: a fixed list of steps that the computer follows the same way every time. A person wrote each of these six recipes. They all ask the same question, are more cells lining up right now than chance explains?, but they follow different steps. The biggest difference is where each one looks to learn what chance is:

algorithmwhat it countswhere it learns what chance looks like
rate+contextevents per second, from all cells added upthe average of the nearby minute (no copies)
CoactDetectcells lit in each 2-second pieceshifted copies of the nearby minute
LoCocells lit in each 1-second pieceshifted copies of the minute before and the minute after
binned SCEcells lit in each 10-second pieceshifted copies of the whole stretch being studied
locustcells "switched on" in each 0.1-second frameshifted copies of the whole recording
SPIKE-synchhow close each event is to events in other cellsno copies: each cell's own gaps between events

Looking nearby means the bar rises when cells get busy, so chance lineups in a busy stretch are not called. The cost is that a small real event in a busy stretch can be missed too. Looking at the whole recording gives one bar for everything, so a busy stretch can pass it again and again. Figures {{NUM:fig_alg_rate}} to {{NUM:fig_alg_sync}} show each recipe on the same made-up recording. Each figure has two views: a quiet minute with one real event (A), and a busy minute where nothing real was added, so any call there is a false alarm (B).

7. The neural networks

The other four programs were not written as recipes. Each one is a small neural network: a stack of simple arithmetic steps, each with numbers the computer can adjust. We showed it many made-up recordings where we knew the answer. After each example, the computer nudged the numbers so the network's output went up at real coordinated events and down everywhere else. That nudging is called training. When training ends, the numbers stay fixed, and the network works like any other program.

The network's design is called tube. Imagine looking down a dark tube while the recording slides past. A coordinated event is a bright flash. Figure {{NUM:fig_network}} follows one recording through each stage of the network.

There are four versions. tube is the one drawn above. tube-guard ignores the brightness right next to the moment it scores, so a real event does not push up its own comparison. tube-ratio divides by the surrounding brightness instead of subtracting it. tube-ratio-guard does both. All four were trained only on made-up recordings, and they were taught that busy stretches never hold a real event.

8. Test recordings with a known answer

To grade a program we need recordings where we know every real event. So we built a program, the simulator, that makes up recordings in the same form as the lab's event lists (Figure {{NUM:fig_simulator}}). Each made-up recording has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells and lasts {{T.minutes}} minutes. It is built in layers:

  • Background: each cell fires on its own, at its own rate. A few cells are busy and many are nearly silent, as in real recordings. We made two versions: a quiet background (about {{T.quiet_per_hour}} events per cell per hour) and a busy background (about {{T.busy_per_hour}}). Those match the quieter and busier untreated recordings in the lab's data ({{T.real_p25_per_hour}} and {{T.real_p75_per_hour}} per hour).
  • Planted events: {{T.n_planted}} coordinated events that we put in on purpose, joined by 30%, 18% or 10% of the cells. The cells in one event start a little apart, not at exactly the same moment. Planted events are at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart.
  • A busy stretch: from minute {{T.hot_from}} to minute {{T.hot_to}}, every cell gets about {{T.hot_per_hour}} extra random events per hour. Nothing is planted there, so any call there is a false alarm.
  • Decoys: {{T.n_decoys}} lineups built exactly like planted events but left off the answer key. They stand in for lineups a careful person would not count. No program that sees only event times can tell a decoy from a real event, so even a perfect program could score only about {{T.ceiling}}.

What the simulator gets wrong. Its busiest cells bunch their events together far more than real busy cells do. Its large lineups are rarer than real ones. It always has {{T.n_cells_sim}} cells, while real recordings have from about half that to more. And we are not sure how spread out real events are in time. So a good score on made-up recordings does not promise a good score on real ones.

9. How we grade a program

We compare each program's calls with the answer key (Figure {{NUM:fig_grading}}). A call that lands within {{T.tol}} seconds of a planted event is a hit. A planted event with no call is a miss. Any other call is a false alarm, including a call on a decoy.

From those we get two numbers: the share of planted events a program found, and the share of its calls that were right. The overall score combines them into one number from 0 to 1. It is high only when both are high. (Its technical name is the F1 score.)

Most programs have one main setting that raises or lowers their bar. To choose it fairly, we made {{T.n_recordings_scored}} test recordings and split them into {{T.n_tests}} groups of {{T.per_test}}. For each group, we picked the best setting using the other {{T.n_choose}} recordings, then scored the program on the {{T.per_test}} it had never seen. The neural networks were trained the same way, never on the recordings they were scored on.

10. Scores on the test recordings

On the quiet background, five programs scored about the same (Figure {{NUM:fig_scores}}): CoactDetect {{T.f1q_coact}}, LoCo {{T.f1q_loco}}, tube-guard {{T.f1q_tube_guard}}, tube {{T.f1q_tube}} and rate+context {{T.f1q_rate}}. The best possible was about {{T.ceiling}}. With this many test recordings we cannot say that any one of them is truly better than the others. binned SCE looks worse ({{T.f1q_sce}}), but most of that comes from how its calls are timed: when we score it over its whole 10-second pieces, it reaches {{T.sce_fair_quiet}}. locust ({{T.f1q_cicada}}) and SPIKE-synch ({{T.f1q_sync}}) scored lower.

Busy stretches are where the programs split apart. The test recordings never hide a real event inside the busy stretch, so a program that goes quiet there looks good even if it has gone blind. To check, we made {{T.block_recordings}} new recordings with real events planted inside the busy stretch (Figure {{NUM:fig_busy}}).

  • CoactDetect and LoCo, which judge chance from nearby time, stay quiet in the busy stretch, but they also miss most real events there: CoactDetect found {{T.out_coact}} of mid-sized events outside it and {{T.in_coact}} inside.
  • locust and binned SCE, which judge chance from the whole recording, keep calling in the busy stretch: about {{T.fa_cicada}} and {{T.fa_sce}} false alarms per minute. binned SCE calls all the way through it, so its calls there tell us nothing.
  • rate+context did both jobs fairly well at this level of busyness ({{T.in_rate}} found inside, {{T.fa_rate}} false alarms per minute). When we made the stretch three times busier, none of the programs held up.

11. The programs on real recordings

A real recording has no answer key. The figures in this section show where each program made calls. They cannot show whether a call is right.

The lab tests two drugs. TTX (tetrodotoxin) blocks the fast electrical signals most nerve cells use. Senktide switches on a sensor these cells carry, the neurokinin 3 receptor (Navarro and others, 2009). Many recordings end with high potassium, which makes healthy cells active. One might expect TTX to stop all teamwork between cells, which would give us a ready-made answer key. In this lab's recordings it does not: events and lineups continue under TTX. So TTX cannot serve as a "no coordination" test.

The lab marks analysis windows: the stretches of each recording it studies. Six of the ten programs run only inside those windows. LoCo and locust run on the whole recording.

12. Where your eye and the programs disagree

Looking at Figures {{NUM:real_ttx_brief}} to {{NUM:real_senk_long}}, two things stand out. Some sharp stripes that anyone can see are missed by most programs. And some calls sit where the raster shows nothing special. We looked at both closely.

First we needed a fair rule for "a stripe anyone can see". We counted a clear stripe when at least {{T.stripe_pct}} of a recording's cells start an event within 1 second, and that is at least twice as many as in the busiest seconds of the two minutes around it. The second part matters. In a busy stretch, a quarter of the cells often line up by chance, and nobody looking at the raster would point to those.

13. What this means

  • No program is shown to be best. On made-up recordings, five score about the same.
  • Busy cells change every program's answer. Some go blind in busy stretches, some call all through them. A drug that makes cells busier can change the calls even if teamwork between cells has not changed. Any study of a drug has to separate the two.
  • A call means "unusual for this recording", not "big". In a quiet recording, 3 cells lining up can be rare enough to call. That is correct for the math and unconvincing to the eye.
    • {{PROSE:meaning_real}}

What could be wrong here

  • Every score comes from made-up recordings, and the simulator is imperfect (Section 8).
  • We ran no statistical test between the top programs, and the decoys cap every score.
  • The neural networks were taught that busy stretches never hold a real event, which may be why some of them go blind there.
  • The "clear stripe" rule in Section 12 is our own. A person may see stripes it misses, or miss some it counts.
  • The real recordings shown are one per group of mice, per drug. That is too few to say how common any of this is.
  • Planted events are always at least {{T.min_sep_min}} minutes apart. That hides a known weakness of the programs that judge chance from nearby time: two real events close together can raise each other's bar.
